## Rules

**Demo-request page anatomy and conversion benchmarks**

1. Set B2B landing-page conversion targets per vertical, not against one blended "B2B average" — Unbounce's own data puts SaaS at a 3.8% median and commercial/professional-services at 6.2%, a 60%+ spread inside "B2B." [LAW] — Unbounce
2. Treat a 2.9–6.6% conversion range as the realistic band for a B2B landing page, and treat anything claiming a flat single-digit-precision "the B2B average is X.X%" without a stated vertical as a red flag in a vendor report. [LAW] — Unbounce (median 6.6% all-industry, 3.8–12.3% by vertical)
3. Write landing-page copy at a 5th–7th grade reading level. Pages at this level converted at 11.1%, 56% higher than pages written at an 8th–9th grade level, in Unbounce's 41,000-page sample — this applies to B2B pages as much as B2C despite the instinct to sound "enterprise." [LAW] — Unbounce
4. Budget for a real mobile-conversion penalty even on B2B pages: mobile converted ~8% worse than desktop despite driving 83% of visits in Unbounce's dataset — do not assume a B2B, desktop-skewing buyer persona makes mobile QA optional. [LAW] — Unbounce
5. Treat published third-party demo-conversion "benchmarks" (1.5–4% baseline, 8–15% top-decile) as directional planning inputs, not committed targets — the source (First Page Sage) could not be independently re-verified this session; anchor real targets to your own vertical's Unbounce-class data instead. [TREND-2026] — First Page Sage (unverified this pass)
6. Track demo-to-qualified and qualified-to-meeting as two separate funnel metrics, not one blended "demo conversion rate" — RevenueHero's proprietary bands (60–70% demo-to-qualified is "healthy," 90%+ is "elite"; 50–60% qualified-to-meeting is "typical") are useful as a funnel-health check even though the underlying sample/methodology isn't fully published. [TREND-2026] — RevenueHero

**Form length vs. qualification tradeoffs**

7. Treat every additional form field as an explicit, named tradeoff between lead volume and lead qualification — decide and document it per page, rather than defaulting to "more fields = more qualified" as a free assumption. [LAW] — HubSpot (directional finding from 40,000+ landing pages: added friction fields measurably reduce conversion)
8. Prioritize removing specific field *types* over blindly cutting field *count*: age, phone number, and geographic detail (especially street-level address) fields carry outsized conversion cost relative to other fields in HubSpot's own tested set. [LAW] — HubSpot
9. Match form depth to traffic intent, not to internal sales-ops wishlist: a first-touch/TOFU form should ask for the minimum needed to start a relationship (name + work email); a bottom-funnel demo request can justify more fields because the visitor has already signaled high intent by reaching that page. [FRAMEWORK] — cross-source consensus (Unbounce vertical data, TrustRadius self-serve data, HubSpot field-type findings)
10. Do not cite a specific "conversion drops X% per added field" curve (e.g., "-4.1% per field," "3 fields=25%, 8 fields=12.5%") as a sourced fact in customer-facing material — this exact figure could not be located in HubSpot's own published post despite being widely attributed to it; see Open Questions. [FRAMEWORK] — this research pass

**Gated vs. ungated content economics**

11. Reserve full gating for genuinely bottom-funnel, high-perceived-value assets (ROI calculators, proprietary benchmark data, live demo access) — content whose value to the visitor clearly exceeds the cost of a form fill. [FRAMEWORK] — cross-source industry consensus (multiple agency sources converge on this funnel-stage framework; no single primary study underlies the framework itself)
12. Leave top-of-funnel, educational content ungated to preserve organic search reach and inbound linkability — gating trades reach for lead capture, and that trade only pays off when the asset's perceived value is high enough to justify the friction. [FRAMEWORK] — cross-source consensus
13. Treat login-walled/gated content as structurally invisible to AI answer engines: content behind a form cannot be crawled, and content that cannot be crawled cannot be cited in AI-generated answers — factor this into the gate/ungate decision as AI-mediated discovery grows, independent of any specific percentage-lift claim. [TREND-2026] — directional finding across GEO/AI-search literature; the specific "64% less likely to be cited" figure attached to this claim in secondary sources is unverified — see Open Questions.

**Multi-stakeholder buying-committee content (champion enablement)**

14. Design B2B content for a committee of roughly 6–13+ people, not a single buyer persona — Forrester's directly-verified 2026 figure is 13 internal stakeholders plus 9 external influencers per typical B2B purchase decision, rising further for complex/strategic deals. [LAW] — Forrester
15. Do not anchor deliverables to one fixed "the committee has N people" number across all deal sizes — Gartner's own practitioner poll shows self-reported committee size ranging roughly 5–16 across up to 4 functions, meaning committee size scales with deal complexity and company size rather than holding constant. [LAW] — Gartner
16. Build at least one internally-shareable, no-further-login artifact per deal (a one-pager, ROI calculator, or comparison matrix) that a champion can forward in an internal Slack/email thread without asking a colleague to fill out another form — an asset that requires re-gating to view can't do its job inside the buying committee. [FRAMEWORK] — champion-enablement literature (directional, cross-source; no single controlled study underlies the specific tactic)
17. Ship content addressed to the recurring buying-committee roles (economic buyer, champion/internal advocate, technical evaluator, end-user, procurement/security gatekeeper) rather than one undifferentiated "why buy us" page — content that maps to zero of these roles serves no one on the committee well. [FRAMEWORK] — cross-source consensus
18. Expect internal committee conflict as a design input, not an edge case: 74% of B2B buying teams reportedly demonstrate "unhealthy conflict" during the decision process — champion-enablement assets exist specifically to help one advocate pre-align the group before that conflict derails the deal. [LAW] — Gartner (confidence note: confirmed via convergent search excerpts, not a direct fetch of the press release itself)
19. Treat larger, more diverse buying groups as a net positive to design for, not just a friction source: 94% of buyers in groups of six-plus reported clearer decision-quality advantages (broader perspectives, shared validation effort, easier budget approval) in Forrester's directly-verified data. [LAW] — Forrester

**Security/compliance trust surfaces (SOC 2 / ISO)**

20. Do not park the SOC 2/ISO badge in the footer only — place at least one instance near an active decision point (primary CTA, pricing section, or a dedicated security/trust section between features and pricing) where a security-sensitive buyer is actually forming a trust judgment. [FRAMEWORK] — Vanta/AICPA guidance
21. Maintain a standalone trust/security page (not just an inline badge) that lets a security reviewer self-serve the compliance answer without contacting sales — this aligns with the broader B2B self-serve preference data (81% of buyers want to find information, including pricing, on their own). [FRAMEWORK] — Vanta guidance; TrustRadius self-serve data
22. Follow AICPA's mark-usage rules exactly when displaying a SOC 2 badge: keep it visually intact and correctly proportioned, and hyperlink it to the AICPA verification page — an altered or unlinked badge is a compliance and brand-credibility risk, not a purely aesthetic choice. [LAW] — AICPA/Vanta
23. Treat security/compliance trust surfaces as most load-bearing for mid-market-and-up B2B SaaS and near-irrelevant for low-commitment/self-serve B2C purchases — this follows directly from the buying-committee-size and gatekeeper-role research above (procurement/security gatekeeper is a recurring committee role in B2B, with no B2C equivalent). [FRAMEWORK] — synthesized from Forrester/Gartner committee-role data

**Pricing-page transparency vs. contact-sales**

24. Publish real pricing (or a clear starting-price anchor) for at least entry/self-serve tiers, and reserve "Contact Sales" for genuinely custom/enterprise tiers — 81% of B2B buyers say they want to find pricing information on their own, from a 2,185-respondent TrustRadius survey. [LAW] — TrustRadius
25. Treat a sitewide "Contact Sales, no visible pricing" pattern as a deliberate filter that trades self-serve conversion for gated lead volume — make that trade explicitly per tier rather than defaulting to it site-wide out of habit or competitive mimicry. [FRAMEWORK] — synthesized from TrustRadius self-serve data
26. Design the pricing page as one of the primary places B2B buyers self-serve their evaluation, not merely a final-step formality — this follows from the ~100% self-serve-preference finding: pricing opacity works against how the majority of the buying committee already wants to operate. [LAW] — TrustRadius

**Proof formats (logos, case studies, peer-review widgets)**

27. Lead with named case studies carrying quantified outcomes (not generic testimonials) on demo-request and pricing pages — B2B buyers need a shareable number to justify the purchase to the rest of their buying committee internally. [FRAMEWORK] — cross-source consensus, consistent with the buying-committee research above
28. When traffic and tooling allow, segment social proof (which logos/case studies show) by the visitor's own inferred vertical or company size rather than showing one static logo wall to everyone — DocSend's segmented-by-visitor-type social proof (startup logos to startup visitors, VC logos to VC visitors) is reported at a 260% conversion lift, though this is a single-company case study, not a replicated benchmark. [TREND-2026] — Mutiny/DocSend case study
29. Use G2's officially-supported embeddable widgets (Grid, Star Rating, Testimonials, Review Snapshot, Review Spotlight) rather than manually screenshotting reviews — they carry structured data that can also surface star ratings directly in search-engine results, which manual screenshots cannot. [FRAMEWORK] — G2 documentation
30. Reserve G2's Review Snapshot widget (curated set of up to 5 reviews) specifically for account-based-marketing pages built for named target accounts, per G2's own stated use case for that widget — it is not designed as a general-purpose homepage element. [FRAMEWORK] — G2 documentation

**Intent-stage differences vs. B2C**

31. Write B2B primary CTAs as conversation-starters ("Book a demo," "Talk to sales," "Request access"), not as purchase-closers ("Buy now") — B2B intent is deliberative and runs through a multi-person committee, unlike a single-decision-maker B2C impulse purchase. [FRAMEWORK] — cross-source consensus
32. Build distinct pages per funnel stage (awareness/thought-leadership, mid-funnel education, bottom-funnel demo/signup) for B2B traffic rather than one generic page serving all visitors — this follows directly from the gated-vs-ungated funnel-stage framework above and from the buying committee's multi-touch, research-heavy journey. [FRAMEWORK] — cross-source consensus
33. Lead B2B social proof with business-outcome case studies and named enterprise logos; lead B2C social proof with volume signals (review counts, star ratings, user-generated content) instead — the credibility currency differs by buyer type because a B2B buyer must justify the purchase to colleagues, while a B2C buyer is typically justifying it only to themselves. [FRAMEWORK] — cross-source consensus

## Anti-patterns

- **Treating "add more form fields" as a free way to improve lead quality.** Every field is a named tradeoff between volume and qualification (Rule 7); HubSpot's own tested data shows specific field types (age, phone, geography) carry disproportionate conversion cost (Rule 8). Adding fields without naming what you're trading away is the anti-pattern, not field count itself.
- **Citing a precise per-field conversion-decay curve (e.g., "-4.1% per field") as a sourced fact.** This figure is widely repeated across secondary/content-mill sources attributing it to "a 2024 HubSpot study," but it does not appear in HubSpot's own published post. Repeating it as fact propagates an unverifiable — likely fabricated — statistic. See Open Questions.
- **Fully gating top-of-funnel educational content by default.** This trades away organic reach and AI-answer-engine citability for a lead-capture benefit that top-of-funnel content, by definition, hasn't yet earned (Rules 11–13).
- **Parking the SOC 2/ISO badge only in the footer and calling the trust surface "done."** Footer-only placement wastes the badge as a conversion asset at the point where a security-sensitive buyer is actually forming a judgment (Rule 20).
- **Defaulting site-wide to "Contact Sales" with no visible pricing anywhere.** This ignores that 81% of B2B buyers in TrustRadius's own survey want to find pricing on their own; make the opacity trade explicit per tier rather than reflexive (Rules 24–25).
- **Building one generic landing page for every stage of the funnel.** B2B's longer, multi-stakeholder, research-heavy journey needs stage-specific pages (Rule 32); a single page trying to serve a first-touch visitor and a bottom-funnel demo-ready buyer under-serves both.
- **Showing one undifferentiated logo wall/testimonial set to every visitor when segmentation is technically available.** Leaves conversion on the table relative to segmented social proof (Rule 28) — though note this specific lift number comes from one company's case study, not a controlled multi-site test; validate on your own traffic before treating the exact percentage as a target.
- **Copy-pasting a statistic from an SEO content-mill blog because it's phrased with high precision (e.g., "67.8%," "4.1%," "64%").** Precision is not the same as verification. Several such figures in this space could not be traced to any primary source during this research pass — see Open Questions for the specific list.

## Open questions

- **"Each additional form field reduces conversion by an average of 4.1%," attributed to "a 2024 HubSpot study."** Checked HubSpot's own blog post on this exact topic directly — the post confirms HubSpot analyzed 40,000+ landing pages and found specific field *types* (age, phone, geography) hurt conversion, but contains **no such percentage figure**. This number appears nearly verbatim across many unrelated secondary/content-mill domains with no working link back to a primary HubSpot publication. **Flagged as unverifiable, likely fabricated.**
- **"3 fields ≈ 25% conversion, 5 fields ≈ 21%, 8 fields ≈ 12.5%," also attributed to HubSpot's 40,000-page analysis.** Same issue as above — the real, confirmed HubSpot analysis exists, but these specific percentage figures could not be located in HubSpot's own post. **Flagged as unverifiable.**
- **"A 2025 Formstack study of 1,500 B2B decision makers found 67.8% form abandonment when more than 7 fields are requested; phone-number fields cause -57% conversion; budget fields cause -41%."** Could not locate this study on formstack.com or in any Formstack press release. The identical wording (down to the "1,500 B2B decision makers" phrasing) appears verbatim across numerous unrelated content-mill domains — a strong signature of a shared, uncorroborated "citation" being copy-pasted rather than independently reported. **Flagged as unverifiable, likely fabricated.**
- **"Forrester Research (2024): the optimal number of form fields for B2B lead generation is 3–5."** Forrester's actual, directly-verified 2024/2026 published research concerns buying-committee stakeholder counts (13 internal + 9 external), not a forms-field-count recommendation. No primary Forrester source for a "3–5 fields" claim was found. **Flagged as likely misattribution/fabrication** — do not cite Forrester for this specific claim.
- **"Gated content is 64% less likely to be cited by AI systems like ChatGPT or Google's SGE."** The underlying mechanism (gated/login-walled content can't be crawled, so it can't be cited) is logically sound and broadly supported by general GEO/AI-search commentary. The specific "64%" figure, however, has no traceable primary study behind it in this research pass. **Use the directional claim; flag the specific percentage as unverified.**
- **"DocSend increased conversions 260% by adding customer logos to their landing page."** As sometimes summarized in secondary sources, this drops the actual mechanism. The real reported tactic (per Mutiny's case description) was *segmenting* logos and copy by visitor type — startups saw startup logos, VCs saw VC logos — not simply adding logos to a static page. The distinction matters for anyone trying to act on the finding. Also note this is a single-company case study, not a replicated benchmark.
- **"Single-CTA landing pages convert at 13.5% vs. 10.5% for pages with 5+ CTAs, from an analysis of 18,639 landing pages."** This is a long-standing, widely-cited figure historically associated with Unbounce/WiderFunnel-era research. It could not be re-confirmed on Unbounce's current live Conversion Benchmark Report page during this session's direct fetch (that report's public content centered on reading-level and mobile/desktop findings instead). Plausible and historically real, but **not independently re-verified this pass** — treat as directionally useful, not as a freshly-sourced number.
- **"76% of B2B buyers say website design influences their purchasing decision" (attributed to BigCommerce).** Could not locate this specific figure in a BigCommerce study. Adjacent but distinct claims exist elsewhere (e.g., roughly 75% of website credibility being design-driven, ~94% of first impressions being design-related) that trace more plausibly to web-credibility research (in the lineage of Stanford's Web Credibility Project) than to BigCommerce specifically. **Flagged as likely misattributed; do not cite BigCommerce for this number.**
- **First Page Sage's B2B demo-request conversion benchmarks (1.5–4% baseline, 8–15% top-decile).** WebFetch was blocked by a domain-safety/network restriction rather than a content error, so this could not be independently confirmed or debunked this session — it remains a widely-cited industry figure of unknown primary rigor. Recommend a follow-up fetch attempt (different tool/session) before treating it as load-bearing.
- **CXL's landing-page infrastructure playbook.** Blocked on direct fetch (HTTP 403) both for the specific article used in this research and, separately, in a prior research pass on ecommerce CRO in this same curriculum (`ecommerce-cro.md`) — CXL appears to actively block this fetch tool. No claims from CXL were used to support any rule in this file as a result; a genuinely CXL-sourced perspective on B2B landing-page structure is a real gap in this research.
- **Gartner's committee-size figure is genuinely inconsistent across Gartner's own public materials** — different secondary sources citing Gartner variously report "6 to 10," "11 to 15," "5 to 16," and "11 to 20" stakeholders, likely reflecting different Gartner studies/years/deal-complexity segments rather than one canonical number. This file cites Gartner only for the range-and-variability point (Rule 15) and defers to Forrester's single, directly-verified 2026 figure (13 internal + 9 external, Rule 14) wherever a specific number is needed.
