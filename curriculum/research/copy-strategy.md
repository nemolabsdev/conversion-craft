## Rules

1. **[Positioning]** Before writing any hero copy, run April Dunford's 5-component positioning exercise in order: (1) list true competitive alternatives (what the customer would do/buy if you didn't exist), (2) list unique attributes vs. those alternatives, (3) translate each attribute into customer-facing value, (4) name the best-fit customer characteristics that care most about that value, (5) pick the market category that frames the value correctly. Skipping straight to headline-writing without this sequence produces vague copy. [LAW] — aprildunford.com
2. **[Positioning]** Pressure-test the chosen market category by asking: "would this headline still make sense if I swapped in a much broader or much narrower category label?" If yes, the category choice is too vague to anchor the reader's comparison set. [LAW] — aprildunford.com
3. **[Awareness]** Identify the primary traffic's awareness stage (Unaware / Problem-Aware / Solution-Aware / Product-Aware / Most-Aware) before drafting the headline; cold, unaware/problem-aware traffic requires a problem-first hook with no product name in the first line, while product-aware/most-aware traffic (retargeting, brand search) can lead with product name + differentiator + offer. [LAW] — kingeshop.com (Eugene Schwartz)
4. **[Awareness]** For Product-Aware and Most-Aware segments, move objection-handling and comparison content (vs. named competitors) above the fold; for Unaware/Problem-Aware segments, put objection-handling below an educational hook instead. [LAW] — kingeshop.com
5. **[Page structure]** Default section order for a primary sales page: Nav → Hero (headline + subhead + primary CTA) → Social proof → secondary CTA → Features/benefits (each handling one objection) → repeat CTA → FAQ/objections → footer. Deviating should be a deliberate choice, not a default. [LAW] — julian.com (Julian Shapiro)
6. **[Headline]** Every headline must pass this test: "if a visitor reads only this line, do they know exactly what is being sold and to whom?" Reject headlines that could be pasted onto a competitor's page unchanged (e.g., "Improve your workflow"). [LAW] — julian.com
7. **[Headline]** Assume roughly 80-90% of visitors read the headline (and CTA) and never reach body copy — cited as "8 of 10 read the headline, 2 of 10 read the rest" (Copyblogger, via Neil Patel) and "90% read headline + CTA" (SEO Sherpa). The headline must carry the complete value proposition, not just curiosity bait. [LAW-directional / TREND-2026 for exact %] — neilpatel.com, seosherpa.com
8. **[Hero]** Keep the subheadline to 1-2 sentences maximum, doing exactly two jobs: state what the product is, and explain why the headline's claim is credible. If it runs to a paragraph, cut it. [LAW] — julian.com
9. **[Hero]** Apply the Desire − (Labor + Confusion) lens to every hero-section element: for each line/button, ask whether it increases desire (value, intrigue), or whether it adds unnecessary labor (reading, scrolling, thinking) or confusion (unclear next action) — cut or rewrite anything that only adds labor/confusion. [LAW] — julian.com
10. **[Features]** Cap the primary page at 3-6 feature blocks. Each block = one benefit-stated header (not a feature label) + one short paragraph that ties feature → benefit → objection handled + one supporting product image or GIF (not stock/abstract art). [LAW] — julian.com
11. **[Framework — FAB]** When writing a feature block, use the Feature → Advantage → Benefit ladder and end on the benefit: state the factual spec (Feature), then the comparative edge it creates (Advantage), then the emotional/practical payoff for the reader (Benefit). Do not stop at Feature or Advantage. [LAW] — thecopybrothers.com
12. **[Framework — BAB]** For transformation-style offers, structure the hero or opening section as Before (1 sentence naming the current pain, specific and relatable) → After (1 sentence naming the achieved state) → Bridge (the product positioned as the mechanism connecting them). [LAW] — thecopybrothers.com
13. **[Claim specificity]** Replace vague superlatives ("the best," "revolutionary," "seamless") with a checkable number, named comparison, or named mechanism. Test: could a competitor paste this claim onto their own page unchanged? If yes, it's too vague to keep. [LAW, moderate confidence — exact "falsifiability" framing not independently re-verified this session] — marketingexamples.com
14. **[Concrete language]** Write claims in visualizable language the reader can picture as a scene, object, or number, rather than abstract nouns ("innovative solutions," "seamless experience"). [LAW, moderate confidence] — marketingexamples.com
15. **[CTA copy]** Lead CTA button copy with a specific action verb plus a stated deliverable, not a generic verb: e.g. "Get my template" or "Find gym and get membership" rather than "Submit" or "Learn more." A cited PPC test moved from "Get membership" to "Find gym and get membership" (adding relevance) for a 68% lift. [TREND-2026] — unbounce.com
16. **[CTA copy]** Prefer benefit-forward verbs over cost-forward verbs in CTAs: a B2B office-rental test found "Get" outperformed "Order" by 38.26%, because "Get" frames what the user receives rather than what they must do. [TREND-2026] — unbounce.com
17. **[CTA copy]** Test first-person CTA phrasing ("Start my trial") against second-person ("Start your trial") rather than assuming a fixed lift — the widely-cited "90%" first-person figure could not be reproduced from Unbounce's own case-study page in this session and should be treated as an upper-bound outlier, not an expected result; run it as an A/B test, not a blanket rule. [TREND-2026, low confidence — see Open Questions] — unbounce.com
18. **[Forms]** Cut form fields to the minimum required for the immediate transaction; cited data associates dropping from 11 fields to 4 with a 120% conversion lift. Every field beyond what's needed to deliver the next step is a labor tax. [TREND-2026] — seosherpa.com
19. **[Offer focus]** Ship one primary offer and one primary CTA per page. Multi-offer/multi-link pages measurably underperform single-offer pages in cited benchmarks (magnitude varies by source, 13.5% vs 10.5% single-link in one dataset, ~266% relative lift for single-offer in another) — treat direction as reliable, magnitude as source-dependent. [TREND-2026] — seosherpa.com
20. **[Readability]** Write body copy at roughly a 5th-7th grade reading level; cited benchmark shows 11.1% conversion at that level vs. 5.3% for college-level copy on comparable offers. Run copy through a readability checker before shipping. [TREND-2026] — seosherpa.com
21. **[Copy length]** For cold/top-of-funnel traffic, default to short-form copy over long-form; cited benchmark is 14% (short) vs 11% (long) conversion. Validate per-audience — long-form copy still tends to outperform for high-consideration, high-price, or Most-Aware-stage offers (unresolved trade-off, see Open Questions). [TREND-2026] — seosherpa.com
22. **[Mobile]** Write and test hero copy at a 375px-wide viewport: headline, subhead, and primary CTA must all be visible without scrolling. Cited mobile share of landing-page traffic is ~83%. [TREND-2026] — seosherpa.com
23. **[Objection sequence]** Map the reader's objection sequence before drafting page copy and answer objections in this cognitive order: (1) legitimacy — "is this real?", (2) relevance — "does this work for my situation?", (3) mechanism — "how does it actually work?", (4) results — "are the numbers real?", (5) peer validation — "what do others say?", (6) pricing/terms transparency, (7) risk — "what if I get burned?" A page that only answers 2-3 of these converts only the warmest visitors. [LAW] — instantpress.co
24. **[Objection placement]** Place legitimacy and relevance signals (logos, named testimonials, "built for [named persona]" language) within the first screen; place mechanism/results detail mid-page; place risk-reversal copy (guarantee, refund policy, free trial terms) directly beside or below the final CTA, not buried in an FAQ only. [LAW] — instantpress.co, julian.com
25. **[FAQ as objection tool]** Every FAQ entry must map to one specific unresolved objection from the sequence in Rule 23 — treat the FAQ block as a targeted objection-handling tool, not a generic-info dump ("What is your company?", "Where are you located?" type filler). [LAW] — instantpress.co
26. **[Social proof placement]** Place at least one social-proof element (logo bar, single named testimonial, or aggregate rating) above the fold; place deeper proof (full case studies, longer testimonials) mid-page. Do not stack multiple redundant proof types back-to-back in the same scroll position. [LAW] — julian.com, wisernotify.com
27. **[Social proof format]** Use named, attributed testimonials (real name + title/company, photo where possible) rather than anonymous quotes. A cited WikiJobs test found adding three short testimonials lifted purchases 34%. [TREND-2026] — wisernotify.com
28. **[Social proof specificity]** Use precise, non-rounded numbers in proof copy ("11,247 businesses," not "thousands of customers"); specificity is reported to read as more credible in social-proof research. [TREND-2026, directional] — wisernotify.com
29. **[Social proof — reviews]** Where you can display third-party reviews (G2, Capterra, Trustpilot, or on-site reviews), do so — a cited Spiegel Research Center (Northwestern) figure associates displaying reviews with up to a 270% conversion lift versus no reviews shown. [TREND-2026] — wisernotify.com
30. **[Social proof — live activity]** If using live-activity/recent-purchase notifications ("Sarah in Austin just signed up"), only show real, recent, verifiable events tied to an actual timestamped log — one cited case (Nicholas Scalice) reported a 32% signup lift from adding real notifications. Fabricated or looped activity feeds are a trust risk, not a proof asset (see Anti-patterns). [TREND-2026] — wisernotify.com
31. **[Pricing — charm]** Use charm pricing (prices ending in .99 or .97) as the default for price-sensitive, transactional/low-consideration offers; cited lift range is roughly 24-35% vs. round-number pricing (MIT/U Chicago 2003, Cornell studies). [TREND-2026] — shno.co
32. **[Pricing — premium]** For premium/luxury positioning, prefer round numbers ($50, $100) over charm pricing — round prices are reported to signal quality/premium positioning rather than value-hunting, and using .99 endings on a premium product can undercut the positioning from Rule 1. [TREND-2026] — shno.co
33. **[Pricing — anchoring]** When displaying tiers, always show a visible reference point next to the target tier — either a higher list price with strikethrough ("was $X, now $Y") or a higher-priced tier alongside it. Cited anchoring effect on perceived value is roughly +32%. [TREND-2026] — shno.co
34. **[Pricing — decoy]** When using a decoy pricing tier, make it a genuinely inferior option priced at or near the target tier's price (same price, strictly worse value), placed directly beside the tier you want chosen. Ariely's Economist test: without the decoy, 68% chose the cheap digital-only option and only 32% chose the bundle; with the decoy present, 84% chose the bundle. Effect size is reported to shrink with realistic (non-lab) stimuli — validate with your own A/B test rather than assuming the lab magnitude transfers. [LAW, with replication caveat] — growmeorganic.com, shno.co

## Trends 2024-2026

- **First-person CTA pronoun testing.** Popularized as a CRO best practice via widely-cited (but inconsistently reproduced) case studies; direction (first person often wins) is plausible but magnitude claims vary wildly (single-digit to 90%+) across sources. Expected shelf life: 2-3 years as a testable heuristic before overuse flattens the effect (once most competitors use "my," the differentiation disappears). Always A/B test rather than ship blind.
- **Live/real-time social proof notification widgets** (e.g., "X just signed up" popups). Growing adoption 2023-2026; effectiveness data is real but thin, and regulatory attention on fake urgency/fake scarcity (FTC dark-pattern enforcement, EU digital fairness rules) is rising. Expected shelf life: 1-2 years before backlash and/or regulation forces stricter authenticity requirements; only use with genuinely real, timestamped events.
- **Reading-level simplification (5th-7th grade copy) driven by mobile-first, short-attention-span browsing.** Backed by both the seosherpa benchmark and the general mobile-traffic-share trend (~83%). Expected shelf life: durable, 3-5+ years — this tracks a structural shift (mobile reading context) rather than a fad.
- **Charm-pricing under increasing scrutiny in regulated markets.** The EU and some US states have moved toward disclosure/dark-pattern rules touching psychological pricing tactics; the "24-35% lift" figures come from pre-regulatory-scrutiny studies (2003-era MIT/Chicago). Expected shelf life for the tactic as currently practiced: uncertain in regulated jurisdictions, likely stable in the US general market for 3+ years.
- **AI-personalized/dynamic on-page copy** (headline or CTA variants served per visitor segment) is an emerging 2025-2026 practice layered on top of the classic awareness-stage segmentation (Rule 3) — not directly covered by scout sources, flagged here as a gap worth separate research if the skill needs dynamic personalization guidance.

## Anti-patterns

- **Award-site hero patterns that delay the value proposition.** Awwwards/CSS-award-style pages frequently open with abstract animation, oversized WebGL/video heroes, or scroll-jacking before any headline text renders — this directly violates Rule 6-9 (headline must be immediately legible) and inflates load time, which independently hurts conversion (cited: pages loading ~2.4s convert roughly 2x better than slower ones; 0.1s speed improvement ≈ 8-10% mobile conversion lift, per seosherpa).
- **Vague, category-less headlines** ("Reimagine the future of X," "The platform for everything") — fails Rule 6's descriptiveness test and fails Rule 1's positioning discipline; these read as confident but tell the visitor nothing checkable.
- **Decorative typography that sacrifices legibility** for visual novelty (low contrast, oversized display type with no clear hierarchy) — works against Rule 7/20 (visitors skim; if the headline is hard to read fast, the 80-90% headline-only readers get nothing).
- **Testimonial carousels that auto-rotate** before a visitor can read a full quote — undermines Rule 27's point of using attributed, readable proof; if used, pause on hover/tap and default to a slow rotation or static grid instead.
- **Fake urgency and fake scarcity** (countdown timers that reset on refresh, "only 3 left in stock" that never changes) — directly contradicts Rule 30's requirement that live-activity proof be real; erodes the legitimacy objection (Rule 23, objection #1) instead of resolving it, and carries rising regulatory risk (see Trends).
- **CTA copy hidden behind scroll-triggered reveals or buried under decorative content** — increases Labor and Confusion in Julian Shapiro's formula (Rule 9); the primary CTA should be reachable without hunting.
- **Generic, low-commitment CTA verbs** ("Submit," "Learn More," "Click Here") — fails Rule 15's specific-verb-plus-deliverable standard.
- **Price hidden behind "Contact us" for low-consideration/self-serve offers** — reintroduces the pricing-transparency objection (Rule 23, objection #6) rather than resolving it; reserve "Contact us" pricing for genuinely custom/enterprise deals.
- **Stock photography of generic people in suits/handshakes** — the opposite of Rule 10's requirement for real product screenshots/GIFs; signals generic marketing rather than a specific, credible product.
- **Walls of unstructured text with no subheads, bullets, or bolding** — works against Rule 7 and Rule 20; visitors who skim (the majority) get nothing to latch onto.
- **Popups/interstitials that block content on first load, before the visitor has read the headline** — adds Labor/Confusion before any Desire has been established; if used at all, delay by scroll depth or exit-intent rather than firing on load.

## Open questions

- **Harry Dry's "three rules" (falsifiability, uniqueness, visualization) framing.** Could not be independently confirmed from a directly fetched marketingexamples.com page in this session (his interview page didn't mention it; his site's formula/psychology sub-pages returned server errors). The underlying principles (specificity, differentiation, concrete language) are consistent with his broadly known teaching style, but the exact "three rules" packaging and the Dollar Shave Club case-study attribution should be treated as unverified pending a working fetch of marketingexamples.com.
- **First-person CTA lift magnitude.** The scout's "90% lift" figure did not appear in this session's direct fetch of the cited Unbounce page (which instead surfaced different tests: "Get" vs "Order" +38.26%, relevance-added CTA +68%). The 90% figure likely originates from a different, often-cited case (commonly attributed to Michael Aagaard/ContentVerve-style tests) that wasn't reachable this session. Treat 90% as an outlier ceiling, not a typical result — always A/B test locally.
- **SEO Sherpa's landing-page statistics page returned materially different figures across scout's summary vs. this session's direct fetch** (e.g., "80% read only headline" vs. "90% read headline+CTA"; single-link 13.5%/10.5% vs. single-offer +266%; no reading-grade-level or link-count data surfaced in the direct fetch at all). This suggests the source is a statistics-aggregator page pulling from many secondary citations inconsistently, or that fetch summarization sampled different sections each time. Recommendation: treat every specific magnitude sourced from this page as directional only, and re-verify against a primary study before using in any claim made to end users.
- **WiserNotify's specific video-testimonial (63% / 3.91%→6.38%) and live-notification (98%) lift figures, and the 4.0-4.7-star "sweet spot" claim,** were not reproduced in this session's direct fetch (which instead surfaced Spiegel Research Center +270%, WiserNotify's own +150%, Nicholas Scalice +32%, WikiJobs +34%). Both sets of figures may coexist on the page at different scroll depths, but only the figures listed in Rules 27-30 above were directly confirmed this session.
- **Decoy-effect framing correction.** The scout's original phrasing ("84% toward bundle vs. 68% without decoy") is corrected in Rule 34: without the decoy, 68% chose the *cheap* option and only 32% chose the bundle — 68% was never the bundle's share. The scout's noted "2014 replications found the effect diminishes with realistic stimuli" caveat was not independently re-verified this session (worth sourcing directly, e.g. Ariely's own later commentary or a specific replication paper, before treating it as settled).
- **April Dunford's full 10-step positioning sequence.** The fetched aprildunford.com page gave the 5-component breakdown and confirmed the existence of a "positioning canvas" output, but did not enumerate all 10 process steps verbatim; the ordering used in this document (competitive alternatives → unique attributes → value → best-fit customer → market category) reflects the widely-documented structure of the 5 *components*, which is solid, but a step-by-step 10-part *process* (assemble team, test with sales, layer in trends, etc.) should be re-sourced directly from the book or a verified secondary summary before treating specific step numbering as gospel.
- **Neil Patel's "hyphen/colon headline +9% CTR" (Content Marketing Institute) claim** was not found in the fetched neilpatel.com article this session and should be treated as unconfirmed until traced to the original CMI source.
- **Eugene Schwartz awareness-stage page-structure mapping.** The primary cited source (kingeshop.com) was unreachable in this session (connection refused); the five-stage model itself is well-established from Schwartz's *Breakthrough Advertising*, but the specific page-structure prescriptions in Rules 3-4 rely on the scout's summary and general knowledge rather than a source re-verified this session.
- **Short vs. long copy trade-off (Rule 21).** Sources agree short copy wins on average for cold traffic, but none of the fetched sources quantified where the crossover point is (price level, consideration complexity, or awareness stage at which long-form starts winning) — this is a genuine open question for the skill to resolve empirically rather than by rule.
