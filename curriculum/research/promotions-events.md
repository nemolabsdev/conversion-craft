# Promotions & Sale-Event Architecture: Banners, Discount Codes, Bundles, Urgency, and Price Integrity

## Sources

## Rules

**Sitewide banners vs. single-primary-CTA discipline**

1. Treat a sitewide sale banner and a page's primary CTA as two messages competing for the same finite attention budget, not as additive information — Unbounce's Attention Ratio framework names 1:1 (one link, one conversion goal) as the target for any single-offer page; a second persistent promotional message pulls the ratio away from 1:1 by definition. [FRAMEWORK] — Unbounce
2. Give the sitewide banner a visually distinct, deliberately lower-contrast treatment than the primary CTA (thinner bar, muted background, no competing button styling) — content that visually resembles an ad or occupies a traditional ad/banner position gets tuned out regardless of relevance; eyetracking studies found such zones capturing as little as 0.8% of fixations against a much larger share of on-page space. Making the banner louder than the primary CTA doesn't win more attention, it just teaches users to filter both. [FRAMEWORK] — NN/g
3. Never run the sale message as an auto-rotating carousel slide competing with other slides — 46% of ecommerce sites that implement homepage carousels ship them with usability defects, and rotating content is doubly vulnerable to banner blindness on top of the base ad-zone effect; if the sale is the priority message, it should occupy the first/only static slot, not a rotation. [FRAMEWORK] — Baymard
4. If a promo bar and a hero CTA must coexist, resolve the hierarchy with position and size, not color alone: keep the promo bar thin and above the fold's primary content (never overlapping or directly adjacent to the CTA button), and keep only one element on the page at "primary button" size/weight at any given scroll position. [FRAMEWORK] — Unbounce, NN/g
5. Do not let the sale banner's copy restate or compete with the primary CTA's verb — the banner should communicate the offer (what/how much/until when), and the CTA should communicate the action (shop/buy); duplicating action language in both places is what produces the "says everything, communicates nothing" failure mode this rule set is designed to prevent. [FRAMEWORK] — Unbounce

**Discount-code entry and apply UX**

6. Collapse the promo/discount-code field behind a "Have a promo code?" link by default rather than showing an empty, always-visible input in checkout — an exposed empty coupon field primes users to feel they're overpaying and to go hunt for a code before completing checkout, even ones who arrived with no such intent. [FRAMEWORK] — Baymard
7. Keep the explicit "Apply" button on promo/gift-code fields — this is the one checkout-field type where Baymard's own auto-save research names an exception: unlike other checkout fields (which should auto-update without a button, since 22% of leading sites requiring "Apply" there causes users to overlook the button and lose data), users already expect an apply step for codes and are far less likely to miss it. [FRAMEWORK] — Baymard
8. After a code is applied, show an explicit, itemized confirmation (code name, discount amount, and the updated total) rather than relying on the user to notice the total changed — the same research names this as necessary specifically because it's the one field where a hidden state change is likely, precisely because users expect an action step there. [FRAMEWORK] — Baymard
9. Auto-apply any promotion the customer is already eligible for (email capture, cart value, first-order) instead of requiring manual code entry when eligibility is already known — removes the primary reason a code field needs to exist in the first place. [FRAMEWORK] — Baymard
10. Budget for code-entry failure as a conversion-critical error state, not an edge case: in a 2023 Bizrate Insights/Digital Commerce 360 survey of ~1,000 online shoppers, nearly 3 in 10 said a promo code failing to apply had frustrated them in the past year — treat "invalid/expired code" messaging and recovery path (e.g., surfacing why it failed, or a live-chat/support link) as a required checkout state, not a generic error toast. [TREND-2026] — Bizrate Insights/Digital Commerce 360

**Bundles and gift-with-purchase merchandising**

11. Set gift-with-purchase (GWP) or free-shipping thresholds roughly 15–30% above current average order value — this range recurs consistently across ecommerce-merchandising practitioner guidance as the band that's reachable without being trivial, though note in Open Questions that this convention comes from commerce-app vendor content rather than an independently replicated study. [FRAMEWORK] — practitioner convention (unverified primary study)
12. Surface GWP/threshold progress before the cart, not only at checkout — place a progress indicator on the product page (near add-to-cart) and in the mini-cart, stated as a concrete remaining amount ("Add $14.50 more for your free gift") rather than a vague nudge, so the threshold can influence the add-to-cart decision itself, not just checkout behavior. [FRAMEWORK] — practitioner convention
13. Keep the GWP qualification rule expressible in one sentence with no conditional branches (one threshold, one gift, one cart-wide rule) — multi-tier or category-restricted GWP rules that need a tooltip to explain are a comprehension tax that erodes the "free" framing's simplicity advantage. [FRAMEWORK] — practitioner convention
14. When bundling, always display the sum of individual item prices next to the bundle price so the discount math is visible, not just asserted — an unstated bundle "discount" with no visible reference math gives the customer nothing to anchor the savings claim against. [FRAMEWORK] — practitioner convention
15. Avoid bundling a low-price-point item with a substantially higher-price-point item as the bundle's primary value driver — pairing a cheap add-on with an expensive anchor item risks the inverse effect of dragging the anchor item's perceived value down toward the cheap item's, rather than lifting the bundle's perceived value up. [FRAMEWORK] — practitioner convention (directional finding; magnitude unverified — see Open Questions)

**Countdowns and truthful temporary urgency**

16. Never use a countdown timer that resets, restarts, or is otherwise disconnected from a real, enforced expiration — the FTC's 2022 dark-patterns report specifically names "baseless countdown timers" (its example: a timer reading "Offer ends in 00:59:48" that doesn't actually gate the offer) as an actionable deceptive practice under FTC Act Section 5. [LAW] — FTC
17. Do not display scarcity claims ("only 1 left in stock," "10 people are looking at this item right now") unless the number shown is a live, accurate read from real inventory/session data — the same FTC report names fabricated or unsubstantiated scarcity messaging alongside countdown timers as a dark-pattern category, evaluated by whether a reasonable consumer would be misled about a material fact affecting the purchase decision. [LAW] — FTC
18. If a countdown is tied to a real sale-event end date, make the timer's expiration the actual, enforced cutoff for that price — when the timer hits zero, the price must change (or the offer must actually close); a timer that is cosmetic once expired converts a truthful urgency device into the exact deceptive pattern the FTC has flagged. [LAW] — FTC
19. Do not bundle mandatory fees into a "reveal at checkout" pattern under sale-event pressure ("drip pricing") — the FTC's dark-patterns guidance separately flags withholding mandatory charges until the final checkout step as deceptive, and a time-pressured sale UI is exactly the context where a shopper is least likely to notice or object to a late fee reveal. [LAW] — FTC

**Sale-event page anatomy and discount depth**

20. Do not default to maximal discount depth as the primary sale-event lever — Klaviyo's BFCM 2025 benchmark (aggregated across its ecommerce customer base) found average sitewide discount depth *fell* from 29.6% to 26.2% year-over-year while same-site sales still grew 11% YoY, with repeat-purchase growth (+14%) outpacing new-buyer growth (+9%) — evidence that loyalty/personalization levers substituted for discount depth without a revenue cost that period. [TREND-2026] — Klaviyo
21. Calibrate discount depth to price tier rather than using one sitewide percentage: in the same Klaviyo data, low-AOV brands' sales performance peaked in the 20–29%-off band, while mid- and high-AOV brands peaked deeper, at 30–39% off — a single flat "20% off everything" banner is measurably off-target for at least one of those tiers. [TREND-2026] — Klaviyo
22. If running a multi-day sale event (e.g., Thanksgiving through Cyber Monday), expect and can deliberately design for a discount-depth peak mid-week rather than on the headline day — in the same report, Small Business Saturday carried the deepest average markdown (~28%) versus Thanksgiving (~24%) and Black Friday itself (~26%), so "Black Friday" as a page name doesn't have to mean "deepest discount day" operationally. [TREND-2026] — Klaviyo
23. Keep the sale-event landing page to a single primary offer framing above the fold (a single headline discount, a single primary CTA) even if the underlying sale has multiple tiers or categories — apply the same Attention Ratio/banner-blindness logic used for the sitewide-banner-vs-CTA case (Rules 1–5) to the sale page itself; a sale page that tries to headline every sub-promotion at once reproduces the same competing-message problem at page scale. [FRAMEWORK] — Unbounce, NN/g

**Strikethrough/was-pricing rules**

24. In the US, only use a "was" or strikethrough former price that reflects an actual, bona fide price the item was offered at "openly and actively... for a reasonably substantial period of time, in the recent, regular course of business" — an artificially inflated reference price used to manufacture a larger-looking discount is explicitly named a "fictitious" former price and a deceptive practice under 16 CFR § 233.1. [LAW] — FTC
25. Do not advertise a nominal, trivial reduction as if it were a genuine bargain — the FTC's own worked example is a "Reduced to $9.99" claim against a $10.00 former price: technically true, but understood by a reasonable consumer to signal "a much greater, and not merely nominal, reduction," making the claim itself misleading regardless of literal accuracy. [LAW] — FTC
26. In California specifically, a former-price/"was" claim must reflect the prevailing market price within the 3 months (90 days) immediately preceding the ad, or the ad must clearly and conspicuously state the date that former price last applied — this is a stricter, explicitly time-boxed standard than the federal "reasonably substantial period" language, and it names "regularly," "originally," "was ___ now ___," and "___% off" as the specific phrasings it governs. [LAW] — Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17501 / 4 CCR § 1301
27. In the EU/EEA, any advertised price reduction (a "was/now" pair, a percentage-off claim, or promotional language implying an advantageous price) must reference the lowest price the trader charged for that item in the 30 days immediately preceding the reduction — not the price the day before the sale started, and not a price cherry-picked from earlier in that window if a lower price occurred later in it. This is now confirmed by the CJEU as applying to percentage-off and "advantageous price" claims generally, not only to an explicit strikethrough pair. [LAW] — EU Directive 98/6/EC Art. 6a; CJEU Case C-330/23 (Aldi Süd)
28. Calculate and store the EU 30-day reference price per sales channel independently (online storefront, physical store, marketplace listing each get their own 30-day lowest-price history) — the Commission's guidance treats channel-specific pricing as the norm, so a single global "lowest price" figure computed across channels is not the compliant reference. [LAW] — European Commission guidance on Directive 98/6/EC Art. 6a
29. The EU 30-day reference-price requirement attaches at the point of sale (e.g., the product detail page showing the strikethrough), not to blanket promotional messaging with no product-level price shown — a sitewide banner reading "This week only: 20% off everything" does not itself need to display a 30-day reference price if no specific former/now price pair is shown there; the obligation activates where an actual price comparison appears. [LAW] — European Commission guidance on Directive 98/6/EC Art. 6a
30. Exempt perishable goods and goods that have been on the market under 30 days from the full 30-day lookback (Member States may set a shorter reference period for these categories) — do not apply a blanket 30-day rule to every SKU without checking whether it qualifies for this carve-out. [LAW] — EU Directive 98/6/EC Art. 6a

**Post-sale price integrity**

31. Treat "was" pricing as a standing legal-exposure surface, not a one-time creative decision — JCPenney ($50M), Kohl's (~$6.15M), and Overstock.com (~$7M to state regulators) each paid real settlements over allegations that advertised "regular"/"compare at" prices were rarely or never the actual selling price; the common fact pattern across all three was systemic, sitewide use of an inflated reference price rather than an isolated mistake. Any recurring strikethrough-pricing program should be treated with the same audit rigor as a financial control, not left to marketing discretion. [LAW] — enforcement precedent (JCPenney/Kohl's/Overstock cases)
32. Keep a durable, queryable price-history log per SKU per channel (not just the current and immediately-prior price) — both the US "regular course of business" former-price standard and the EU 30-day lowest-price rule require being able to reconstruct and defend a specific historical price on demand; a system that only stores "current price" and "displayed was-price" cannot substantiate either standard if challenged. [LAW] — FTC 16 CFR 233.1; EU Directive 98/6/EC Art. 6a
33. Do not silently reprice items back up immediately after a sale event ends if the "was" price used during the sale was itself the pre-sale price — reverting to a price identical to the "was" price advertised during the sale is consistent and defensible; reverting to a *higher* price than the advertised "was" price invites the inference that the "was" price itself was inflated for the sale (the exact fact pattern in the JCPenney/Kohl's/Overstock cases). [LAW] — enforcement precedent
34. If offering a post-purchase price-adjustment/price-protection window (refunding the difference if the price drops shortly after a customer buys), state the window length and eligibility rule explicitly and enforce it consistently — this is a trust-building feature specifically because it's inconsistently offered across the industry (e.g., Amazon has no formal universal policy while other major retailers do), so an unstated or inconsistently-honored policy converts a trust signal into a support-ticket liability. [FRAMEWORK] — industry practice survey (see Open Questions on primary-source strength)

## Anti-patterns

- Sitewide promo banner styled and sized to compete with (or outweigh) the primary CTA button — produces the exact "no primary message" hierarchy failure this research set is meant to prevent (Rules 1–5).
- Auto-rotating hero/homepage carousel used to fit "the sale banner, the new-arrivals banner, and the seasonal banner" into one slot — near-half of real-world implementations already have usability defects even with a single promo slide, and rotation compounds the banner-blindness risk on top of that (Rule 3).
- Exposed, always-visible "Enter discount code" field on the checkout page as a default state — primes fee-neutral customers to feel they're overpaying and to leave the funnel to search for a code (Rule 6).
- Discount-code field with no visible "Apply" confirmation state, or an "Apply" button that looks identical to (and gets confused with) the primary "Continue"/"Place Order" button (Rules 7–8).
- Countdown timer that resets on page reload, or continues to display a live countdown after the nominal deadline has passed with no actual price/availability change (Rule 16, 18) — this is FTC-named, not just a UX nitpick.
- "X people are viewing this" / "Only 2 left!" scarcity messaging driven by a static or randomized value rather than live inventory/session data (Rule 17).
- Strikethrough "was" price that was never the actual, sustained selling price of the item — whether inflated the day before a sale, or never charged at all (Rules 24–27, and the JCPenney/Kohl's/Overstock precedent in Rule 31).
- One global "lowest price in 30 days" computed across all sales channels rather than tracked per-channel, when operating in the EU/EEA (Rule 28).
- Reverting a post-sale price to a level higher than the "was" price shown during the sale itself (Rule 33).
- GWP or bundle offer with a qualification rule complex enough to require a tooltip or FAQ link to explain (Rule 13).
- Bundling a cheap, low-relevance item with a premium anchor item purely to hit a bundle-discount aesthetic, with no visible per-item price breakdown to justify the bundle math (Rules 14–15).

## Open questions

- The oft-cited "GWP campaigns lift conversion 15–25% and AOV up to 30%," "anchor pricing increases perceived value 22–35%," and "psychological pricing converts 15–35% higher" figures could not be traced to a named, methodologically transparent primary study during this research pass — they recur across ecommerce-app-vendor content marketing (Shopify-app blogs, bundle-tool vendors) with no consistent underlying source cited, and in several cases the same range appears reused across unrelated claims. Treat these specific magnitudes as unverified/likely-inflated marketing claims, not as benchmarkable targets, until a primary source is found.
- A widely-circulated claim that "61% of consumers have stopped purchasing from a brand due to deceptive pricing," attributed to the "2025 Edelman Trust Barometer," could not be located anywhere in Edelman's actual 2025 Trust Barometer or Special Report on Brand Trust after direct searching of Edelman's own site and several secondary write-ups of that report. This appears to be a fabricated or badly misattributed statistic originating from a single SEO content site (smartsmssolutions.com) and should not be cited or reused.
- A frequently-repeated stat that "46% of shoppers abandon their cart when a discount code fails to apply" (seen on Drip.com and similar cart-abandonment roundup blogs) could not be traced to a named primary study within this pass — the fetch of the page citing it did not return a verifiable underlying source. The more conservative, source-traceable figure in this report is the Bizrate Insights/Digital Commerce 360 "nearly 3 in 10 frustrated" survey result (Rule 10); prefer that figure over the 46% one until the latter is independently confirmed.
- The "set GWP/free-shipping threshold 15–30% above current AOV" convention (Rule 11) is consistent across multiple independent ecommerce-merchandising and bundle-app vendor sources, which is suggestive, but none of the sources checked cite a controlled study — this should be read as converged practitioner folklore, not as an experimentally validated number. Recommend treating it as a testable starting hypothesis per store, not a fixed rule, and pairing it with the experimentation-measurement research for a proper A/B validation protocol.
- No Baymard-caliber large-sample usability-benchmark study specific to product bundling or gift-with-purchase UX (equivalent to their carousel, filter, or checkout-field research) was located. Bundle/GWP rules in this report (11–15) are therefore tagged [FRAMEWORK] at the level of "converged practitioner convention," a weaker evidentiary tier than the [LAW]-tagged pricing-regulation rules or the Baymard-sourced banner/carousel/code-field rules — flag this gap explicitly to whoever consumes this research; it's a good candidate for a dedicated primary study or internal A/B program rather than a borrowed statistic.
- This pass did not verify whether individual US states beyond California (e.g., New York's GBL § 218, or state-level "junk fee"/drip-pricing statutes) impose additional strikethrough-pricing requirements beyond the federal FTC Guides — flagged as a gap for legal review before treating Rule 26's California standard as the binding US floor nationwide.
- The EU guidance notice (Commission Notice C/2021/9320) itself could not be fetched directly in this pass (the EUR-Lex page did not return machine-readable body text to the fetch tool); Rules 27–30 rest on corroborating secondary legal-analysis sources (RPC Legal, Talon.One, Noerr) rather than a direct read of the Commission's own notice text. Recommend a follow-up direct read of the EUR-Lex PDF (CELEX:52021XC1229(06)) before treating any single numeric detail in Rules 27–30 as final for a compliance-critical implementation.
