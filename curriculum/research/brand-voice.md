## Rules

### Foundational frameworks

1. Score your brand's target tone on NN/g's four independent 3-point scales — Funny↔Serious, Formal↔Casual, Respectful↔Irreverent, Enthusiastic↔Matter-of-fact — as four separate decisions, not one "friendly/professional" slider; a brand can be simultaneously casual, respectful, and matter-of-fact, and collapsing the four into one axis loses real distinctions. [FRAMEWORK] — nngroup.com/articles/tone-of-voice-dimensions
2. Document voice and tone as two different artifacts: one voice definition that does not change across the site, plus an explicit table of tone rules that shift only by situation and audience emotional state. Three independent style-guide sources converge on this exact split (Mailchimp: "you have the same voice all the time, but your tone changes"; Atlassian: voice is "consistent," tone "adapts to specific situations"; Shopify: adopts the same distinction explicitly crediting Mailchimp) — treat it as settled practice, not a stylistic preference. [LAW] — styleguide.mailchimp.com, atlassian.design/foundations/content/voice-tone, Shopify (via bizsanford Medium)
3. Cross-check hand-picked voice adjectives against Aaker's five validated brand-personality dimensions (Sincerity, Excitement, Competence, Sophistication, Ruggedness) before finalizing a voice chart — if none of your chosen traits maps to any of the five, confirm you're describing a stable personality trait and not a one-off campaign tone or a values statement mislabeled as voice. [FRAMEWORK] — Aaker (1997), Journal of Marketing Research
4. Structure the voice chart itself around five checkable axes, not adjectives alone: Concepts (which product-principle ideas recur in copy), Vocabulary (an approved/banned term list), Verbosity (a target sentence/paragraph length per surface type), Grammar (sentence-complexity rules), and Punctuation/Capitalization (exact house rules, e.g., Oxford comma yes/no, sentence case vs. title case per element). Each axis must produce a rule a new writer can follow without asking a follow-up question. [FRAMEWORK] — Podmajersky, *Strategic Writing for UX*

### Voice charts & this-not-that pairs

5. Build tone words by opposition, not single adjectives: pick the target trait and pair it explicitly with the adjacent trait you are ruling out — NN/g's worked example for a hospital is "sympathetic but not cheerful" and "professional but also caring." A single adjective like "friendly" is not specific enough for a writer to self-check against; the paired negative is what makes it checkable. [FRAMEWORK] — nngroup.com/articles/tone-voice-words
6. For every core voice trait, write a one-sentence guardrail in the form "[trait] but never [violation]" (e.g., "Funny but never crass," "Confident but never arrogant") and post all guardrail sentences together as the team's single-page quick-reference — this is the practical, shippable version of rule 5 for a whole trait set rather than one word pair. [FRAMEWORK] — brand voice guide practitioner consensus (kaleenastroud.com, toptal.com pattern, cross-checked against NN/g's method)
7. Never finalize a tone-word list or voice chart from internal stakeholder judgment alone. Run a rating study (minimum: a 5-point Likert scale on friendliness and formality, per NN/g's own methodology) with real users before locking the chart — NN/g states plainly: "you are not your users — your interpretation of tone will almost certainly be different from your users' interpretation." [FRAMEWORK] — nngroup.com/articles/tone-voice-words
8. Treat measured effect sizes as the deciding evidence when two tone options are both plausible on-brand: in NN/g's controlled study, shifting to a casual/enthusiastic register moved friendliness ratings by 0.5–0.7 points (5-point scale) but trust ratings by only 0.3–0.4 points, and trustworthiness explained roughly 52% of the variance in "willingness to recommend" — a 2.2x larger effect than friendliness. When a tone choice trades likability against trust, optimize for trust. [FRAMEWORK] — nngroup.com/articles/tone-voice-users, nngroup.com/articles/tone-voice-samples

### Tone modulation — errors

9. Never use blame-framed or clinical-fault words in error copy — "invalid," "illegal," "incorrect," or phrasing that puts the user at fault ("You entered an invalid ZIP code"). Rewrite as system-owned language ("We couldn't find that ZIP code"). This exact rule is independently stated by NN/g, UX Content Collective, and three additional practitioner sources reviewed in this research pass — treat it as converged, not a single org's opinion. [LAW] — nngroup.com/articles/error-message-guidelines, uxcontent.com/how-to-write-error-messages
10. Every error message must state the plain-language cause and the user's next action in the same message — a message that names only the problem without a remedy fails the standard regardless of how well-toned it is. NN/g and UX Content Collective both give this as a non-negotiable structural rule, independent of tone. [LAW] — nngroup.com/articles/error-message-guidelines, uxcontent.com/how-to-write-error-messages
11. Default error-message tone to neutral-to-empathetic, not comedic — NN/g's explicit reasoning is that a joke "can become stale if users encounter the error frequently." Reserve any lightness for genuinely rare, low-severity, first-encounter errors only (e.g., a typo in a search box), never for recurring or blocking errors. [FRAMEWORK] — nngroup.com/articles/error-message-guidelines
12. Calibrate apology intensity to a 3-tier severity scale rather than a fixed phrase: low severity (typo, minor validation) → light and brief ("Looks like a typo — try again"); moderate severity (failed action, retry needed) → reassuring and solution-forward ("That didn't go through — let's try again"); high severity (data loss, payment failure, account lock) → no jokes, direct acknowledgment, explicit next step, and a support contact. [FRAMEWORK] — usertesting.com/blog/humanize-error-messages-for-ux
13. Ban ALL CAPS and stacked exclamation marks from error and warning copy regardless of brand enthusiasm level elsewhere on the site — visually "louder" formatting reads as the interface shouting at the user, which undermines the neutral-tone rule above even when the words themselves are compliant. [FRAMEWORK] — uxcontent.com/how-to-write-error-messages

### Tone modulation — checkout, payment & success

14. Suppress jokes and wordplay entirely through the cart-to-confirmation flow, independent of how playful the brand voice is elsewhere on the site — keep language calm, direct, and neutral at every checkout and payment step; reserve humor for earlier-funnel, lower-stakes content (browsing, blog, marketing email). [FRAMEWORK] — weareyellowball.com/guides/micro-copy-ux-words (moderate confidence, single secondary source)
15. In precision-critical confirmation moments — payment succeeded, funds transferred, contract submitted — favor exactness and a clear next step over celebratory language, even for an otherwise playful brand. The cited pattern: Stripe's payment-confirmation screens prioritize precision and next-steps over any celebration, specifically because the job of that screen is trust, not delight. [FRAMEWORK] — bricxlabs.com/blogs/success-message-ux-examples
16. Scale celebration intensity to the significance of the action, not to brand personality alone: reserve full-screen celebration (confetti, large graphics, exclamatory copy) for milestone or rare actions; give routine, frequent actions a subtle, low-key confirmation (a small checkmark, one calm sentence). Using the big celebration on every save both trains users to dismiss it unread and cheapens the real milestones when they occur. [FRAMEWORK] — atlassian.design/foundations/content/designing-messages, bricxlabs.com/blogs/success-message-ux-examples

### Tone modulation — legal, compliance & high-stakes content

17. Write legal, consent, and terms-adjacent copy in plain first-read language, not legalese — replace jargon-first phrasing ("The data controller will process electronic mail addresses for transactional correspondence") with direct plain-language equivalents ("We use your email to send order updates"). Courts and regulators are cited as increasingly treating unreadable disclosure as inadequate/non-binding, which makes this a compliance issue, not a style preference. [LAW] — termsfeed.com/blog/terms-service-labeling-design-readability-act-tldr, usercentrics.com/guides/website-disclaimers/disclaimer-examples
18. Minimize evaluative adjectives and emotive language in official, compliance-adjacent copy (shipping terms, returns policy, cookie/privacy notices, pricing disclaimers) — GOV.UK's official content standard names adjectives explicitly as "subjective" and warns they make text "sound like spin." Target register: "serious but not pompous," "emotionless" rather than persuasive. This directly narrows how much brand-voice personality belongs in these specific blocks (see Open Questions for the tension this creates on a sales page). [FRAMEWORK] — guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/right-tone
19. Drop reflexive courtesy words ("please," "please note") from instructional and legal/compliance copy — per GOV.UK guidance they add length without adding real politeness once the surrounding tone is already respectful. Reserve "please" for a genuine request, not a routine instruction ("Enter your postcode," not "Please enter your postcode"). [FRAMEWORK] — guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/right-tone
20. Reduce warmth and casualness as task stakes rise, even where it departs from the site's default tone — cited research found a friendly register builds trust in low-stakes interactions, but once consequences are high (payment, legal commitment, health/financial decisions), users prefer language that signals accountability and careful reasoning over friendliness. Treat "how much could this specific step cost the user if it goes wrong" as the dial that turns warmth down, not a fixed per-page rule. [FRAMEWORK] — nngroup.com/articles/humanizing-ai, sciencex.com/news/2026-06-friendly-ai-backfire-tone-doesnt
21. Distinguish politeness from humanization at every touchpoint: transactional pleasantries that give closure ("Thanks — that's booked") are safe everywhere, including high-stakes flows; pleasantries that simulate personal interest or relationship ("What are you up to after this?") should be reserved only for contexts where the brand deliberately wants companion-style engagement, and dropped from checkout, legal, and support-ticket copy by default. [FRAMEWORK] — nngroup.com/articles/humanizing-ai
22. Before shipping any cheerful or casual copy on a page discussing a serious-consequence topic (legal changes, financial risk, health, active outages), run the specific check NN/g's own case study failed on: a chatbot opened a page about Brexit's economic consequences with "Hello, I'm Brexit Bot, your friendly guide" — read your first line back and ask whether the register matches the topic's actual weight, independent of whether it matches the brand's general voice. [FRAMEWORK] — nngroup.com/articles/humanizing-ai

### Voice coexisting with conversion-copy clarity

23. When brand-voice wordplay and plain instructional copy compete for the same sentence — a primary CTA, a form label, a price, an error — default to the unambiguous version even when it is less "on-voice." Reserve wordplay for sections where comprehension is not time-critical (hero taglines, empty states, marketing emails), not for the exact moment a user must complete a task. [FRAMEWORK] — synthesis of UX-copywriting-for-conversion sources (duomobranding.medium.com, writitude.com)
24. Adopt Mailchimp's own explicit tie-breaker rule verbatim: tone is usually informal, "but it's always more important to be clear than entertaining." When voice and clarity conflict on one line, clarity wins by the style guide's own stated hierarchy — this is not a case-by-case judgment call to relitigate each time. [FRAMEWORK] — styleguide.mailchimp.com/voice-and-tone
25. Use humor only when it "comes naturally" to the specific line, never inserted to hit a personality quota — Mailchimp's stated reasoning: "forced humor can be worse than none at all." If a joke requires straining to land, cut it rather than keep it purely for voice-compliance. [FRAMEWORK] — styleguide.mailchimp.com/voice-and-tone
26. Never write interface copy in a brand mascot's literal first-person voice — a mascot can appear visually to embody personality without narrating UI text; Mailchimp's own rule states its mascot Freddie "never speaks." Keep the actual functional copy (buttons, errors, form labels) in the brand's plain, human voice, not the mascot's character voice, so clarity isn't filtered through a fictional persona at the exact moments users need instructions. [FRAMEWORK] — raw content-style-guide (mailchimp/GitHub)
27. Turn personality intensity up or down by the user's real-time emotional state rather than applying one fixed tone per page: Atlassian's model turns voice "boldness" and "wink" up when users are confident, curious, or newly successful, and turns it down (more practical, plainer) when users are apprehensive, confused, overwhelmed, or mid-error. Apply the same sliding rule across a single sales page — celebratory tone on a post-purchase confirmation, plainer tone in the payment form two screens earlier. [FRAMEWORK] — atlassian.design/foundations/content/voice-tone

### Verbal distinctiveness — naming, lexicon, cadence

28. Maintain a governed brand lexicon as a living document, not a one-time exercise: a list of (a) proprietary/coined terms unique to the product (Starbucks' "venti," Slack's "huddles"), (b) preferred terms for common concepts, and (c) explicitly banned synonyms. Version it and review it on the same cadence as the design-token or copy-deck update cycle. [FRAMEWORK] — ramotion.com/blog/verbal-identity
29. Before promoting any coined term, tagline, or recurring phrase into permanent brand language, evaluate it on the Distinctiveness Grid's two axes — prevalence (what share of the target audience links the phrase to your brand, unprompted) and uniqueness (how many competitors that same phrase is also linked to). A phrase scoring low on either axis is not yet a real distinctive verbal asset and shouldn't be treated as load-bearing for recognition. [FRAMEWORK] — distinctivebat.com/distinctive-brand-assets (exact scoring thresholds not published on this page — see Open Questions)

### Governance, testing & AI-scaled copy (2026)

30. Reuse the tone-rating instrument for ongoing QA, not just initial voice-setting: periodically sample live copy across surfaces (marketing site, app UI, transactional email, support replies) and score each sample against the four-dimension tone profile from rule 1, the same way you scored candidate copy when the voice chart was first built. [FRAMEWORK] — nngroup.com/articles/tone-of-voice-dimensions (application-framework step 2)
31. Where AI tools draft or scale brand copy, gate every output behind a documented input layer (approved source material + structured brief templates) before generation, and a QA rubric scoring brand-alignment, factual accuracy, and compliance before publication — do not treat a one-time prompt as sufficient voice governance. [TREND-2026] — the-brand-algorithm.com/ai-brand-voice-and-governance
32. Risk-tier AI-assisted copy by consequence before it publishes: low-risk strings (internal notes, minor UI labels) can ship on an automated tone-check pass; medium-risk copy (customer emails, blog posts) needs one human editor; high-risk copy (pricing, legal, crisis communication) needs multi-stage review including brand and legal sign-off. Applying one review bar to all AI-assisted output under-reviews the high-risk cases. [TREND-2026] — the-brand-algorithm.com/ai-brand-voice-and-governance

## Anti-patterns

- **Single-axis tone ("just be friendly").** Collapsing NN/g's four independent dimensions into one vague slider produces a voice chart nobody can apply consistently — "friendly" alone doesn't tell a writer whether to also be formal, funny, or enthusiastic.
- **Adjective-only voice words with no paired negative.** "We're playful" without "but never [X]" leaves the actual boundary undefined; two writers will draw the line in different places.
- **Validating tone choices only with the internal team.** NN/g's own warning — the people who wrote the voice chart are the worst-positioned to judge how it reads to a stranger.
- **Humor in recurring or high-severity errors.** A joke that's charming the first time reads as tone-deaf the fifth time a user hits the same blocking error.
- **Blame-framed error copy** ("You entered an invalid email") — shifts fault to the user and measurably increases frustration versus system-owned phrasing.
- **Cheerful copy on high-consequence topics.** The Brexit Bot pattern: a friendly greeting layered on serious-stakes content reads as tonally dishonest about what's actually at risk.
- **Simulated personal interest in transactional flows** ("What are you up to today?" from a support bot) — invites anthropomorphization the product can't sustain and reduces trust in the answer's accuracy.
- **The same full-screen celebration for every save.** Trains users to dismiss it unread and drains the moment when a real milestone happens.
- **Legalese dressed as "on-brand voice."** Jargon-heavy terms/consent copy isn't just off-tone, it risks being treated as inadequate disclosure by regulators and courts.
- **Mascots narrating first-person UI copy.** Filters functional instructions through a fictional persona exactly when users need unambiguous direction.
- **Forced humor inserted to "sound like the brand."** Strained jokes read worse than no joke — Mailchimp's own explicit warning.
- **AI-generated copy shipped without a voice-input layer or QA rubric.** Defaults to generic, brand-agnostic phrasing that quietly erodes verbal distinctiveness at scale.
- **Treating brand voice and conversion clarity as permanently in tension instead of sequenced** — the resolved pattern across every style guide reviewed is "clarity wins the tie," not "pick one forever."

## Open questions

- **GOV.UK's near-total avoidance of adjectives vs. commercial sales copy's reliance on evaluative language.** GOV.UK explicitly minimizes adjectives as "subjective"/"spin," but persuasive sales copy (per the team's own copy-strategy.md research) leans on concrete, specific claims that are often adjective-carrying. The resolution used above (rule 18) restricts the GOV.UK-style register to compliance-adjacent blocks only — legal, shipping, privacy — not hero/product copy. This boundary should be explicitly confirmed with the team rather than assumed; it wasn't tested empirically in any source reviewed.
- **Ehrenberg-Bass Distinctiveness Grid's exact scoring methodology.** The source fetched describes the two axes (prevalence, uniqueness) and its use for evaluating brand assets, but does not itself publish the numeric thresholds or survey methodology for scoring a candidate verbal asset. Treat rule 29 as directional until a primary Romaniuk source (the *Building Distinctive Brand Assets* book) can be checked directly.
- **Podmajersky's five-axis voice chart** was cross-confirmed only via secondary book-review/summary sources, not a direct fetch of the book text (no public URL). The five axis names are consistent across sources, but their exact operational definitions may differ slightly from the primary text — flag as moderate confidence.
- **Shopify Polaris's own voice-and-tone page is currently dead** (redirects to a generic docs hub, `shopify.dev/docs/api/polaris`, with no equivalent content found there). Everything attributed to Shopify in this file comes from a single third-party summary (Biz Sanford's Medium post) — worth re-verifying if Shopify republishes primary content design guidance.
- **The "40% lower engagement" figure for ungated AI content** (cited in a 2026 trend search result, attributed to "internal testing published by AI CMO") could not be traced to a checkable primary study — treat as a marketing claim, not a verified statistic, and don't quote the number externally.
- **Checkout-tone rule (14)** rests on a single secondary source that could not be directly fetched (403 error) — the direction (no jokes at checkout) matches the broader high-stakes-tone-reduction pattern (rule 20) and so is plausible, but it wasn't independently corroborated by a second source the way the error-message rules were.
- **NN/g's tone-voice-users study (100 participants)** — solid methodology (controlled paired-copy design, three Likert scales) but a single study from a single research org; the effect sizes in rule 8 have not been independently replicated by a second lab in the sources reviewed here.
