# Visual Identity Systems for Sales Websites — Iconography, Ownable Hue, Motion Identity

Distilled research on three visual-identity layers that separate a distinctive brand from a generic template: (1) iconography built as a system rather than a scavenged icon pile, (2) choosing which *hue* a brand owns — not just building a compliant ramp from a hue already chosen — and (3) motion as a recognizable, brand-specific signature rather than generic UI easing. Evidence quality is split: icon-grid specs are exact and normative (they ship in production design systems); color-relationship "rules" are compositional conventions, not laws; Ehrenberg-Bass distinctive-asset research is peer-adjacent marketing science with real (if sometimes hard-to-verify) numbers; brand-motion-identity claims are mostly practitioner case studies, flagged as such.

**Squircles / corner radius as identity**
- [Squircle.js — The Squircle Formula](https://squircle.js.org/blog/math-behind-squircles) and [How Apple Uses Squircles in iOS Design](https://squircle.js.org/blog/squircles-in-apple-design) — iOS app-icon corner radius ≈22.37% of icon width with ~60% corner smoothing (superellipse, not a circular-arc rounded rect); CSS `corner-shape`/`superellipse()` is Chromium-only (~65% global reach) as of mid-2026, not yet Baseline.

**Color relationships (hue selection, not ramp construction)**
- [IxDF — Complementary Colors: The Ultimate Guide](https://ixdf.org/literature/article/complementary-colors-and-color-wheel) — complementary = max contrast (CTA/accent use, avoid for body-text pairs); analogous = harmonious/calming (background/supporting use); triadic = balanced vibrancy; split-complementary = complementary-level contrast with less visual tension, commonly recommended default for brand palettes.
- Cross-search synthesis (colorpickerimage.org, graf1x.com, htmlcolorcodes.com) corroborating the same four-relationship taxonomy and the "pick primary hue → check its analogous neighborhood for supporting colors → check its complement/split-complement for the accent" workflow.
- **Note**: 60-30-10 allocation and OKLCH/12-step ramp construction are already covered in `color-systems.md` — this file covers hue *selection* (which point on the wheel), not ramp *construction* (how one hue becomes 12 steps).

**Category color audits and ownable hue**
- [Overmatter Design — Color Theory for Brand Identity](https://www.overmatter.design/blog/color-theory-for-brand-identity-how-to-choose-more-than-your-favorite-blue) — practitioner methodology: map competitors on the wheel first, identify what the category already "owns" (colors so common they no longer differentiate), then find the gap; Dunkin's pink+orange pairing as a durable, uncopied ownable combination, kept intentionally through a 2019 rebrand.
- Cross-search synthesis (inkbotdesign.com, whatifdesign.co) on a "map top-20 competitors → if ~80% cluster on one hue, that hue is the least ownable place to stand" heuristic, and a named example (OneSpan identifying purple/violet as open space against a category dominated by bright blue) — treated as a single-agency case study, not a controlled study; cite as illustrative methodology, not as a statistic.

**Ehrenberg-Bass distinctive brand assets**
- [Ehrenberg-Bass Institute — Brands of Distinction](https://marketingscience.info/brands-of-distinction/) and [Distinctive Brand Assets Framework — Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/frameworks/marketing-frameworks/distinctive-brand-assets-framework-ehrenberg-bass/) — Jenni Romaniuk's Distinctiveness Grid measures each candidate asset (color, shape, character, sound, motif) on two axes: **Fame** (what share of category buyers link the cue to *any* brand) and **Uniqueness** (what share of that link goes to *your* brand specifically, vs. competitors).
- [Marketing Week — "Only 15% of brand assets are 'truly distinctive', study finds"](https://www.marketingweek.com/15-brand-assets-truly-distinctive-finds-research/) (via cross-search corroboration, direct fetch blocked 403) — Ipsos × Jones Knowles Ritchie (JKR) study, 5,000+ brand assets tested against 26,000+ global consumers, method = show people a color/logo/phrase/font/image in isolation and ask which brand(s) it triggers; found under 1-in-5 assets clear the "truly distinctive" bar. This is an Ipsos/JKR study that *builds on* Ehrenberg-Bass's framework, not an Ehrenberg-Bass Institute study itself — cite the attribution precisely.
- [Mondaq / CLG Global — Tiffany Blue, Cadbury Purple and UPS Brown](https://www.mondaq.com/trademark/1306304/tiffany-blue-cadbury-purple-and-ups-brown-you-can-trademark-your-brand-colour) — Tiffany Blue (Pantone 1837) trademarked 1998, in use since 1845; Cadbury's purple (Pantone 2685C) used since 1914, fought by Nestlé on the theory colors can't be owned, finally secured by UK High Court ruling in 2022 — both confirm color trademarks are real, category-scoped legal protection, not just a design metaphor, but they take decades of consistent, exclusive use to earn.

**Motion identity**
- [Material Design 3 — Motion](https://m3.material.io/styles/motion/) (via cross-search, direct fetch blocked by JS rendering) and [Android Authority — Material 3 Expressive deep dive](https://www.androidauthority.com/google-material-3-expressive-features-changes-availability-supported-devices-3556392/) — M3 Expressive (May 2025) replaced pure duration/easing-curve tokens with spring-physics tokens (stiffness + damping ratio), offered in two named schemes: **Expressive** (low damping, visible overshoot/bounce, for hero moments) and **Standard** (high damping, minimal bounce, subdued) — each with fast/default/slow speed variants for both "spatial" (position/size/shape) and "effects" (opacity/color) motion categories.
- [Apple Developer — `interactiveSpring(response:dampingFraction:)`](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/animation/interactivespring(response:dampingfraction:blendduration:)) and cross-search synthesis (Kodeco/raywenderlich, Hacking with Swift) — default spring: response 0.55 / dampingFraction 0.825; interactive-spring default: response 0.15 / dampingFraction 0.86; lower response = snappier, lower damping fraction = bouncier — these two numeric knobs are the operational lever for a brand's "confident-snappy" vs. "soft-luxurious" motion character.
- [Octopus Marketing Agency — The Language of Brand Motion](https://www.octopusmarketing.agency/blog/the-language-of-brand-motion-conveying-stories-through-movement/) (practitioner source, MIXED confidence) — contrasts "snappy/confident" archetypes (quick elastic entrances, snap-back feedback, e.g. fintech/Mailchimp-style) against "slow/luxurious" archetypes (slow fades, soft easing, extended durations, e.g. wellness/Greenhouse-style); cites Google Material's 200–500ms band and a single unnamed MIT Media Lab reference (0.4s subconscious-judgment threshold) that could not be independently verified in this pass.
- [The Brand Identity — Putting the pin in Pinterest: how DIA brought a bespoke motion system](https://the-brandidentity.com/project/putting-the-pin-in-pinterest-how-dia-brought-a-bespoke-motion-system-to-the-inspiration-platform) — case study of DIA's 2022 rebuild of Pinterest's motion system around a single named, reusable motion behavior ("the Pinterest Push") plus documented sub-components (Pin-Grids, Push-Pins, Collage) so multiple internal/external teams could produce consistent motion without a shared vocabulary breaking down — cited as a concrete existence proof that "one named signature motion + a written toolkit" is a real, adopted practice, not just theory.
- **Note**: generic duration bands (150–600ms), Nielsen's 0.1/1/10s response thresholds, `prefers-reduced-motion`, and Core Web Vitals performance budgets are already covered in `motion-performance.md` — this file covers motion as brand *identity* (which specific curve/spring a brand owns and why), not baseline UI-motion hygiene.

## Rules

### Icon grid systems

1. **Pick one base grid (24×24 is the modern default; IBM Carbon runs a 16/20/24/32 family) and hold every icon in the set to it — never mix icons drawn on different grids in the same UI.** Material Design 3 uses a 24×24dp grid with a 20×20dp live area (2dp of border margin on all sides); Lucide, Heroicons, and Phosphor all standardized on 24×24 as the web/app default. [LAW] (Material Design 3; Lucide; Heroicons)
2. **Fix one stroke weight for the entire set and never mix it with a second weight in the same interface** — Material's regular (400) weight uses a 2dp stroke; Carbon uses 2px at 32px scaling down to 1px at 16px; Lucide defaults to 2px; Bigeye's guide recommends settling on 1.5, 2, or 2.5px and applying it universally. Mixing a 1.5px icon next to a 2px icon reads as sloppy even to a non-designer's eye. [LAW] (Material Design 3; Carbon; Lucide; Bigeye)
3. **Do not mix filled and outlined icon styles in the same view.** Pick one style (stroke/outline or filled/solid) as the default and reserve the other exclusively for a deliberate state signal (e.g., filled = selected/active, outline = unselected) — never as an accidental inconsistency from sourcing icons off two different libraries. [LAW] (designsystems.com)
4. **Set padding equal to (or, for hairline 1px strokes, double) the stroke weight, and pad curved/round icon shapes more than square ones to compensate for optical shrinkage** — a circle and a square of identical bounding-box size read as visually different sizes; the circle needs to be drawn slightly larger to *look* equal. Verify by blurring your eyes ("squint test") across the full icon set at once. [LAW] (designsystems.com; Bigeye)
5. **Corner radius on the icon strokes/caps is a brand-personality lever, not a neutral choice — pair it with the same radius token used on buttons and cards.** 0px (sharp) caps read serious/technical; 2–4px rounded caps read approachable/friendly. A product using an 8px card-radius token but hard-cornered icons is sending two conflicting shape signals in the same viewport. [LAW] (Bigeye)
6. **If your brand mark or UI uses continuous "squircle" corners (Apple's superellipse app-icon shape), replicate the same curve family, not a circular-arc rounded-rect, in any icon container/badge that echoes it** — iOS app icons use a corner radius ≈22.37% of width with ~60% corner smoothing, a mathematically distinct curve from `border-radius`'s circular arc; the visual difference is subtle but consistently perceptible side-by-side. As of mid-2026, CSS `corner-shape`/`superellipse()` is Chromium-only (~65% global reach, not yet Baseline) — treat true squircles as a native-app/SVG-path technique for web, not yet a safe CSS default. [TREND-2026] (Squircle.js)
7. **Adopt optical sizing (a distinct rendering per size band) rather than one master SVG scaled up/down, once icons span more than roughly 2× in rendered size.** Material Symbols ships a 20–48dp optical-size axis that thickens/thins strokes automatically so a 20dp icon and a 48dp icon both look correctly weighted at their own size; SF Symbols solves the same problem with 9 discrete weights × 3 scales (27 renderings) rather than continuous interpolation. A single 24px SVG naively scaled to 48px will look thin and undersized next to type at that size. [LAW] (Material Symbols; Apple SF Symbols)
8. **Enforce one metaphor per icon, two as an absolute ceiling, with the primary metaphor as the icon's sole focal point.** Combining unrelated visual concepts into one glyph is the single most common cause of an icon set feeling "off" — cross-referenced convergence from Microsoft, Apple, and independent UX-writing sources on this exact ceiling. Name icons by what they visually depict ("magnifying-glass", "envelope"), not by the abstract action they trigger ("search-users"), so the name doesn't silently license a second metaphor sneaking into the artwork. [LAW] (Microsoft Windows icon guidelines; Apple HIG; cross-search synthesis)
9. **Default to an established icon library over custom-drawing a new icon whenever a conventional metaphor already exists** (magnifying glass = search, trash can = delete, envelope = mail) — only commission a custom icon after confirming no existing symbol serves the concept, and validate any genuinely new metaphor with real users (free-association testing: show the icon alone, ask what it means) before shipping it into the set. [LAW] (Bigeye)
10. **Choose a library by its license fit before its aesthetic fit.** Lucide (ISC), Heroicons (MIT), Phosphor (MIT) and Material Symbols (Apache 2.0) are all free for unrestricted commercial use with no runtime attribution UI required (license text must still ship in source). Font Awesome Free is commercial-safe but capped near ~2,000 icons (CC BY 4.0 / SIL OFL 1.1 / MIT composite; the paid Pro tier unlocks the rest for $60–$600/yr). The Noun Project's CC BY tier is free but requires visible attribution per icon unless you pay a $4.99 single-icon buyout or hold a Pro/Pro Plus subscription — the free tier is a live legal-attribution obligation, not a truly free asset. [LAW] (Lucide; Heroicons/GitHub; Font Awesome; The Noun Project)
11. **When mixing icons from more than one source library (common when a starter kit's set runs short), redraw the outliers into the dominant library's grid/stroke/cap spec rather than dropping them in unmodified** — a single mismatched icon (wrong stroke weight, wrong corner treatment, wrong grid) is more visible to users than an entire set that's merely "not the trendiest choice." [LAW] (designsystems.com; Bigeye)
12. **Icons must clear the same 4.5:1 (normal) / 3:1 (non-text UI element) contrast thresholds as text and borders — a decorative-looking icon is not automatically exempt.** Carbon's own guidance explicitly holds icons to the same ratio as typography. This is the icon-specific instance of the WCAG rules already detailed in `color-systems.md`; flagged here because icon color is frequently treated as "just decoration" and skipped in contrast audits. [LAW] (Carbon Design System; WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.3/1.4.11)

### Choosing the hue (not the ramp)

13. **Treat the four classical color relationships as accent-selection tools, not ramp-construction tools: complementary (opposite on the wheel) for maximum-contrast CTA isolation; analogous (adjacent) for calm, cohesive backgrounds/supporting surfaces; triadic (three equidistant hues) for balanced vibrancy across a multi-brand or multi-category product; split-complementary (base + the two hues flanking its complement) as the default recommended starting point — same contrast power as a pure complementary pair with less visual tension.** [LAW] (IxDF)
14. **Never use a pure complementary pair (e.g., red text on a green surface, or vice versa) for extended body copy** — the equal-and-opposite saturation of true complements creates chromatic vibration/eye strain at running-text sizes; reserve complementary pairs for small, high-attention elements (a CTA button against its surrounding surface) where the vibration reads as "pop," not fatigue. [LAW] (IxDF)
15. **Derive the accent color from the primary's split-complement or straight complement, and derive supporting/secondary surface colors from the primary's analogous neighbors** — this gives a mechanical, repeatable answer to "what's our accent color" instead of picking one by eye, and it guarantees the accent sits at maximum perceptual distance from the primary for CTA isolation (reinforcing the contrast-wins CTA rule already in `color-systems.md`). [LAW] (IxDF; cross-search synthesis)
16. **Run a competitive hue audit before finalizing a brand's primary color: plot your top 10–20 direct competitors' primary brand hues on a single color wheel and mark which hue(s) the category has already saturated.** If materially more than half your competitive set clusters on one hue family (blue in tech/finance/B2B SaaS is the most commonly cited example), that hue is confirmed as the *least* ownable place to plant a flag, not the safest default. [FRAMEWORK, single-agency methodology — not a controlled study] (Overmatter Design)
17. **Distinguish "category-conventional" from "category-required."** A fintech brand can use blue's trust association *and* still differentiate, by pairing it with an accent no direct competitor has claimed (e.g., navy + copper instead of navy + a second blue) — the strategy is adding a distinctive accent on top of the conventional base, not abandoning the base hue's psychological fit. This directly extends the category-color-convention rule already in `color-systems.md` (Rule 24) into an actionable audit-and-pair workflow. [FRAMEWORK] (Overmatter Design)
18. **A genuinely ownable color pairing, once found, should be locked and defended for years, not seasons.** Dunkin's pink+orange combination has gone uncopied by any major competitor across decades and survived a full 2019 brand refresh unchanged — cited by its own design team as a case of "when you have great brand colors, you don't change them." Treat a validated ownable-hue decision as closer to a legal asset than a seasonal creative choice. [TREND-2026 direction / durable pattern] (Overmatter Design)
19. **Understand that a genuine color trademark (Tiffany Blue, Cadbury's purple) is real, enforceable, and category-scoped — but takes decades of exclusive, consistent use to secure, not a rebrand cycle.** Tiffany Blue (Pantone 1837) was trademarked in 1998 after use dating to 1845; Cadbury's purple (Pantone 2685C) was in use from 1914 and only secured by UK High Court ruling in 2022, after Nestlé's direct legal challenge. Treat "ownable hue" as a multi-year brand-equity investment goal, not a claim you can make after one site launch. [LAW] (Mondaq/CLG Global — UK High Court ruling)
20. **Score any candidate signature color (or shape/character/motif) on two axes before committing brand budget to it: Fame (do category buyers link this cue to *any* brand at all) and Uniqueness (of the people who do link it, what share links it to *you* specifically, versus competitors).** A color can be famous but not unique (everyone recognizes "blue = tech," but it points to no single brand) — only assets that score well on *both* axes function as true distinctive assets. [FRAMEWORK] (Ehrenberg-Bass / Jenni Romaniuk's Distinctiveness Grid)
21. **Expect most candidate brand-identity assets to fail the distinctiveness bar — budget accordingly and prioritize ruthlessly.** An Ipsos × JKR study spanning 5,000+ tested brand assets and 26,000+ consumers (built on the Ehrenberg-Bass framework) found under 1-in-5 (15%) cleared the "truly distinctive" threshold. The practical implication: don't spread a rebrand budget evenly across color + shape + character + icon-style + motion as five co-equal bets — identify which one or two candidate assets are already testing well and concentrate reinforcement there. [LAW] (Ipsos/JKR study, cross-source corroborated; not an Ehrenberg-Bass Institute study directly — attribute precisely)
22. **A distinctive color asset must appear consistently across every touchpoint at sufficient prevalence to build the Fame/Uniqueness link — a color used only on the logo, and nowhere else in the product (buttons, icons, section backgrounds, email templates, ad creative), will not accumulate distinctiveness no matter how "ownable" the hue is on paper.** This is the direct implication of the Fame axis: Fame is built through repeated exposure across contexts, not through a single correct color choice made once. [FRAMEWORK] (Ehrenberg-Bass distinctive-asset framework)

### Motion identity

23. **A brand's motion signature is defined by two numeric knobs, not a vibe: spring stiffness/response (how fast it resolves) and damping ratio (how much it overshoots/bounces before settling) — pick specific values and reuse them everywhere, the same way a brand locks a specific hex value.** Apple's own SwiftUI default spring (response 0.55 / dampingFraction 0.825) reads noticeably calmer than its interactive-spring default (response 0.15 / dampingFraction 0.86); lowering response makes motion feel snappier/more confident, lowering damping fraction makes it feel bouncier/more playful — these are the two levers to tune deliberately per brand character, not leave at framework defaults. [LAW] (Apple Developer — SwiftUI spring API)
24. **Material 3 Expressive formalizes exactly this snappy-vs-luxurious choice as two named, swappable schemes: "Expressive" (low damping, visible overshoot, reserved for hero moments/key interactions) and "Standard" (high damping, minimal bounce, subdued) — each still offered at fast/default/slow speed variants.** Adopt the same two-scheme mental model even outside Android: define your brand's "hero interaction" spring (bouncier, used sparingly on 1–2 signature moments) separately from your "workhorse UI" spring (calmer, used for every ordinary hover/toggle/transition) rather than applying one spring value everywhere. [TREND-2026] (Material Design 3 Expressive, May 2025)
25. **Snappy-confident brand motion (fintech, productivity, B2B SaaS archetypes) pairs low response/duration with low-to-moderate bounce: quick elastic entrances, fast snap-back feedback on interaction, short exit durations.** Slow-luxurious brand motion (wellness, hospitality, premium DTC archetypes) pairs longer duration with near-zero bounce: slow fades, soft ease curves, no overshoot at all — overshoot on a luxury brand reads as "cheap," not "delightful." Choose the archetype deliberately per brand positioning; don't default to whichever spring preset a component library ships with. [MIXED — practitioner pattern, not a controlled study] (Octopus Marketing Agency)
26. **Keep every custom brand-spring choice inside the performance-safe band already established for generic UI motion — a distinctive spring signature is a *character* choice within the 150–600ms / compositor-only-property envelope, not license to exceed it.** A "luxurious" 900ms hero transition that also jank-animates `width`/`top` fails Core Web Vitals regardless of how on-brand the curve looks; see `motion-performance.md` Rules 1–9 for the hard technical ceiling this identity layer must stay inside. [LAW] (cross-reference to `motion-performance.md`)
27. **Name and document your signature motion behavior as a single reusable pattern — not a scattered set of per-component easing overrides — the way DIA's Pinterest rebuild named "the Pinterest Push" as one specific, reusable motion behavior with documented sub-components (grid push, collage rhythm), so any team member or contractor could reproduce it without re-deriving the physics from scratch.** A motion identity that only lives in one senior designer's muscle memory will drift the moment a second person touches the codebase. [TREND-2026, single case study — cite as existence proof of the practice, not as a benchmark to match] (The Brand Identity — DIA/Pinterest case study)
28. **Apply the signature spring/easing consistently to the same *category* of event across the whole site — all entrances use the brand's entrance spring, all exits use its (typically faster, less bouncy) exit spring, all hover feedback uses its micro-interaction spring — rather than hand-tuning each component's animation independently.** This is what turns "an animation" into "a recognizable motion signature": repetition across contexts is exactly the mechanism Ehrenberg-Bass's Fame axis describes for color and shape, applied to motion. [FRAMEWORK] (synthesis: Ehrenberg-Bass Fame mechanism + Material 3 Expressive scheme model)
29. **Reserve the bounciest/most expressive end of your spring range for genuinely rare, high-attention moments (a hero CTA press, a checkout-success confirmation, a first-run onboarding reveal) and keep everyday chrome (nav hover, tab switch, form-field focus) on the calmer end of the same brand's range.** Material 3 Expressive's own guidance explicitly scopes low-damping/high-overshoot springs to "hero moments," not blanket application — overusing the expressive end everywhere flattens it back into generic noise and defeats the signature's purpose. [TREND-2026] (Material Design 3 Expressive)

## Anti-patterns

- **Building an icon set by pulling individual icons from three or four different free libraries without normalizing grid, stroke weight, or corner treatment.** The mismatch (one 1.5px-stroke sharp-cornered icon next to a 2px-stroke rounded one) reads as unpolished even to viewers who can't articulate why.
- **Scaling a single 24px SVG up to 48px+ instead of using an optically-sized variant or a purpose-drawn larger glyph.** The stroke reads as too-thin and the icon looks visually smaller than surrounding 48px UI elements, even though the bounding box matches.
- **Cramming two unrelated metaphors into one icon** (e.g., a gear + a person for "user settings") because no single existing icon felt descriptive enough — this is the top cited cause of icon-set confusion across Microsoft, Apple, and independent UX sources.
- **Treating icon color as exempt from contrast requirements because it "looks decorative."** Carbon and WCAG both hold icons to the same 4.5:1/3:1 thresholds as text/borders; a beautiful icon set that fails contrast on a light background is still an accessibility failure.
- **Using The Noun Project's free CC BY tier in a commercial product without displaying the required attribution**, then discovering the attribution obligation only during a legal/procurement review — budget the $4.99 buyout (or a Pro subscription) up front if attribution UI isn't wanted.
- **Applying a `border-radius` rounded-rect to try to replicate an iOS-style squircle badge or icon container.** The curve families are mathematically different (superellipse vs. circular arc) and the mismatch is visible side-by-side even though it's hard to name — either commit to true squircle SVG paths or accept a standard rounded rect deliberately, don't half-copy the look.
- **Choosing a brand's primary color by personal taste or generic "psychology" lookup table (blue=trust) without first mapping where competitors already sit on the wheel.** A "trustworthy blue" chosen without a competitive audit is statistically likely to land in the most crowded, least differentiating part of the category's color space.
- **Spreading a rebrand budget evenly across five candidate distinctive assets (color, shape, mascot, icon style, motion) instead of testing Fame/Uniqueness and doubling down on whichever one or two already score well.** With roughly 85% of tested brand assets failing to reach true distinctiveness (Ipsos/JKR), even-handed investment across untested assets is a low-odds bet compared to reinforcing a proven one.
- **Introducing a signature color only on the logo and hero section, then reverting to generic neutral grays for every button, icon, and functional UI element.** Distinctiveness is built through repeated exposure across contexts (the Fame mechanism); a color that only appears once per page will never accumulate brand-linkage regardless of how carefully it was chosen.
- **Copying a competitor's or inspiration site's exact spring/easing values (e.g., a specific `cubic-bezier()` lifted from a competitor's CSS) instead of deriving a value that expresses your own brand's character.** This produces a page that *feels* like the competitor's product wearing a different color scheme — motion is exactly the kind of cue Ehrenberg-Bass's framework says builds brand linkage, and copying it directly builds the competitor's linkage on your site, not yours.
- **Applying the "expressive," high-bounce spring preset to every interaction site-wide** (every hover, every toggle, every tooltip) because it demoed well once on a hero button. Material 3 Expressive's own guidance scopes it to rare hero moments; blanket application reads as chaotic rather than confident and burns through the interaction's ability to signal "this moment is special."
- **Letting a "luxurious," longer-duration brand motion signature drift past the 500–600ms hard ceiling or animate layout-triggering CSS properties** in the name of brand character — a jank-y, CLS-inducing "elegant" transition fails Core Web Vitals and actively costs conversions regardless of how on-brand the curve intent was.

- **Material Design 3's exact numeric spring tokens** (specific stiffness and damping-ratio values for the Expressive vs. Standard schemes, at fast/default/slow speeds) could not be extracted — the official m3.material.io motion pages are JS-rendered and returned only page titles to WebFetch. - **The "84% of consumers identify McDonald's from color alone" statistic**, commonly repeated in distinctive-brand-asset commentary, could not be traced to a primary Ehrenberg-Bass or Ipsos/JKR source in this research pass and is deliberately *not* included in the Rules above — treat any version of this specific figure encountered elsewhere as unverified until a primary citation is located.
- **The MIT Media Lab "0.4 seconds for subconscious judgment" claim**, cited by one practitioner motion-identity article (Octopus Marketing Agency), could not be independently verified or traced to a named study — excluded from the Rules section for the same reason.

- **Squircle/superellipse adoption timeline for mainstream CSS** (`corner-shape`, `superellipse()`) is still unsettled — Chromium-only at ~65% reach with no committed Safari/Firefox timeline as of mid-2026; revisit this file's Rule 6 guidance once/if Baseline status changes, since it currently forces a native-app/SVG-only workaround for web use.
- **Whether "signature motion" (Rules 23–29) meaningfully moves conversion or brand-recall metrics on sales pages specifically** — the Ehrenberg-Bass Fame/Uniqueness framework and its supporting Ipsos/JKR statistics were built and tested on color, logos, and packaging; this file *extends* that framework to motion by analogy (Rule 28), which is a reasonable but unproven inference, not a finding the cited sources make directly about motion.
