# Form Design Beyond Checkout & Unified Accessibility for Sales Sites

## Rules

**Form field count & structure**

1. Keep lead-gen forms to the minimum fields needed to qualify and route the lead (typically 3–5 for cold traffic); every field beyond that is a candidate for removal or a later step, mirroring Baymard's own named practice of cutting checkout forms roughly in half (16→8 fields) as a repeatable optimization lever. [FRAMEWORK] — Baymard
2. Treat published field-count-to-conversion percentage curves as directional, not load-bearing: the direction ("fewer fields → higher completion") is consistent across every source reviewed, but no source in this research pass exposed a cleanly-attributed primary dataset for the exact slope — see Open Questions before quoting a specific number in client-facing material. [TREND-2026] — Digital Applied (aggregated, source-mapping incomplete)
3. Move to a multi-step / progressive-disclosure structure once a form needs 4+ fields or asks for qualification detail beyond name and email; keep single-step for ≤3 fields. Treat the specific lift percentages circulating for this pattern (e.g., "86–300% higher conversion") as unverified vendor claims, not confirmed research. [TREND-2026] — practitioner consensus, weak primary sourcing (see Open Questions)

**Labels & placeholder anti-patterns**

4. Place field labels above the input by default (top-aligned); reserve left-aligned inline labels for short forms (≤4 fields) with roughly equal label lengths, since top placement needs about half as many visual fixations and keeps scanning one-directional. [FRAMEWORK] — UX Movement/Wroblewski eye-tracking, Baymard mobile-label study
5. Never use placeholder text as the sole label. Render a persistent, visible label outside the field boundary at all times — placeholders vanish on input, fail short-term-memory recall, and are not reliably announced by screen readers. [LAW] — NNGroup usability testing + WCAG 3.3.2 Labels or Instructions (Level A)
6. If a placeholder is used at all, use it only as a supplementary format hint (e.g., "MM/YY") in addition to a real label — never to convey the field's identity, and never to convey required/optional status. [FRAMEWORK] — NNGroup
7. Programmatically associate every label with its input via `<label for>` or a wrapping `<label>` — this single defect (unlabeled or mis-labeled inputs) is present on 48.2% of the top 1M home pages tested and affects 34.2% of all form inputs, making it the most common forms-specific accessibility failure measured. [LAW] — WCAG 1.3.1 / 4.1.2, WebAIM Million 2025
8. Mark both required and optional fields explicitly (e.g., asterisk + a visible legend for required, an "(optional)" suffix for optional) rather than marking one and implying the other. Only 14% of desktop and 6% of mobile forms do this today, and leaving required fields unmarked causes roughly a third of users (32%, tested) to skip at least one. [FRAMEWORK] — Baymard

**Error message design & validation**

9. Fire inline validation on blur (when the user leaves the field) — never on focus/first keystroke, and never submit-only. Once an error is shown, clear it live at the keystroke level as the user types a valid correction. [FRAMEWORK] — Baymard usability testing
10. Link every error message to its field with `aria-describedby`, and set `aria-invalid="true"` on the offending input so assistive technology announces both the fact of an error and its content. [LAW] — WAI-ARIA `aria-describedby`/`aria-errormessage` technique (MDN, WCAG-aligned)
11. Use `role="status"` / `aria-live="polite"` for non-blocking validation feedback, and reserve `role="alert"` / `aria-live="assertive"` for submit-blocking errors — assertive regions interrupt the screen reader mid-sentence and should not fire for routine field-level hints. [LAW] — WAI-ARIA Authoring Practices
12. On a failed submission, move keyboard focus to the first invalid field, or to a linked error summary at the top of the form — never leave focus on the submit button while errors sit unannounced below the fold. [LAW] — WCAG 3.3.1 technique guidance (G83/G85-style pattern)
13. Write error copy that states what's wrong and how to fix it (e.g., "Enter a valid email like name@company.com"), never a bare "Invalid input." [FRAMEWORK] — accessibility/UX consensus (UXPin et al.)
14. Never use color alone to signal an error state — pair the red border/background with an icon and text so the state survives grayscale/color-blind viewing. [LAW] — WCAG 1.4.1 Use of Color
15. Maintain a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for label, body, and error text, and at least 3:1 for large text (≥18pt, or ≥14pt bold) and input/UI-component borders. Low contrast is the single most common accessibility defect measured on the open web, present on 79.1% of the top 1M home pages. [LAW] — WCAG 1.4.3 / 1.4.11, WebAIM Million 2025

**Login, account creation & password UX**

16. Support clipboard paste and password-manager autofill in every password and confirm-password field — never block paste via script. This is now a direct WCAG compliance requirement, not just a courtesy. [LAW] — WCAG 2.2 SC 3.3.8 Accessible Authentication (Minimum)
17. Do not gate login, signup, or a gated lead-gen form behind a cognitive-function test (a memorized password with no alternative, or a puzzle-only CAPTCHA) with no other path through — provide at least one of: an alternative mechanism, object recognition, or a personal-content check. [LAW] — WCAG 2.2 SC 3.3.8
18. Display password requirements persistently next to the field for the field's entire active state, not only after a failed attempt. [FRAMEWORK] — practitioner consensus (widely repeated, not independently verified against a controlled study this pass)
19. Prefer a visible show/hide password toggle over relying on a "confirm password" field as the primary typo-safeguard — the toggle solves the same problem (mistyped passwords) with one field instead of two. Treat this as informed practitioner consensus rather than a hard-tested finding; the most commonly cited primary source (CXL) could not be independently verified this pass (HTTP 403). [TREND-2026] — see Open Questions
20. Use standard HTML `autocomplete` tokens (`name`, `email`, `tel`, `street-address`, `cc-number`, `one-time-code`, etc.) on every identity, payment, and OTP field so the browser/password manager/AT can identify field purpose programmatically. [LAW] — WCAG 1.3.5 Identify Input Purpose (Level AA)
21. Never require re-entry of information the user already provided earlier in the same process (e.g., re-typing an email confirmed on a prior step) — auto-populate it or offer it as a selectable option. [LAW] — WCAG 2.2 SC 3.3.7 Redundant Entry (Level A)

**Keyboard navigation, focus & modals**

22. Trap focus inside an open modal dialog: Tab and Shift+Tab must cycle only through the modal's own focusable elements, wrapping at both ends, so keyboard users cannot tab into hidden background content. [LAW] — W3C WAI-ARIA APG Modal Dialog Pattern
23. Mark modal containers `role="dialog"` (or `role="alertdialog"` for confirmations) with `aria-modal="true"` and `aria-labelledby` pointing at the visible title; hide everything behind the open modal from assistive tech via `aria-hidden="true"` or the native `inert` attribute. [LAW] — W3C WAI-ARIA APG
24. Move focus into the modal on open (to the title for content-heavy dialogs, or to the first safe interactive control otherwise), and make Escape close it while returning focus to the element that triggered it. [LAW] — W3C WAI-ARIA APG
25. Keep the visible keyboard-focus indicator fully unobscured at every scroll position — a sticky header, sticky footer, or cookie/consent banner must never fully cover a focused element. [LAW] — WCAG 2.2 SC 2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured (Minimum)
26. Provide a "skip to main content" link as the first focusable element on every page, hidden until it receives keyboard focus. [LAW] — WCAG 2.4.1 Bypass Blocks
27. Ship a single-pointer (tap/click, no-drag) alternative for any interaction built on a drag gesture — sliders, reorderable lists, before/after image comparisons. [LAW] — WCAG 2.2 SC 2.5.7 Dragging Movements

**Landmarks, structure & alt text**

28. Wrap every page in exactly one `<main>` landmark plus `<header>`(banner)/`<nav>`/`<footer>`(contentinfo); when a page has more than one `<nav>` or region instance, disambiguate each with a unique `aria-label` (not one that just repeats the role name, e.g. avoid a nav labeled "Navigation"). [LAW] — W3C ARIA11 technique, WAI-ARIA Landmarks guidance
29. Declare page language via `<html lang="...">` — 15.8% of the top 1M home pages still omit it, which breaks correct screen-reader pronunciation and OS-level voice switching. [LAW] — WCAG 3.1.1, WebAIM Million 2025
30. Write product-image alt text around what a buyer needs to decide: product type plus visible, purchase-relevant attributes (material, color, pattern) — not the filename, not generic "product photo." Give purely decorative images `alt=""`; give functional images (icon-only buttons/links) alt text that describes the action performed, not the icon's appearance. [LAW] — WCAG 1.1.1; missing/inadequate alt text is present on 55.5% of home pages tested (WebAIM Million 2025)
31. Size every pointer/touch target at a minimum of 24×24 CSS px (the WCAG compliance floor), but design primary CTAs and form controls to 44×44pt (Apple HIG) or 48×48dp (Material Design) — treat 24×24 as the legal minimum, not the usability target. [LAW] — WCAG 2.2 SC 2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum); Apple Human Interface Guidelines; Google Material Design

**Legal exposure**

32. Budget accessibility remediation as an ongoing legal-risk line item, not a one-time project: 3,117 website-accessibility lawsuits were filed in US federal court in 2025 alone (+27% year-over-year), representing 36% of all ADA Title III federal filings that year, concentrated in New York, Florida, and Illinois. [LAW] — Seyfarth Shaw ADA Title III tracker, 2025 year-end report
33. Treat WCAG 2.1/2.2 Level AA as the de facto compliance bar for a US commercial site, even though Title III itself codifies no technical standard — it is the standard referenced in the DOJ's 2024 ADA Title II rule for public-sector sites and is the benchmark most commonly cited in private Title III demand letters and settlements. [LAW] — DOJ Title II final rule; ADA Title III tracker commentary

## Trends 2024-2026

- **WCAG 2.2 (Oct 2023) is now the working baseline, not a future target.** Its three most form-relevant new criteria — 2.5.8 Target Size, 3.3.7 Redundant Entry, and 3.3.8 Accessible Authentication — are reshaping how lead-gen and login forms get built in 2024–2026 refreshes. Durable, regulation-anchored.
- **Passwordless/passkey adoption is converging with the accessible-authentication requirement.** NIST finalized SP 800-63-4 in mid-2025, formally recognizing passkeys as AAL2-compliant; WCAG 3.3.8 independently bans memory-only authentication as the sole path. Security policy and accessibility law are now pulling in the same direction for the first time. Durable, structural.
- **The EU's European Accessibility Act (EAA) began enforcement June 28, 2025**, extending WCAG-equivalent obligations to many commercial sites/apps selling to EU consumers, not just public-sector ones — widening the legal-exposure conversation beyond the US ADA landscape covered in the Rules above. Durable.
- **Explicit required + optional field marking is still a minority practice (14% desktop / 6% mobile per Baymard) but is trending upward** as more teams respond to the same conversion data. Moderate confidence; still far from standard.
- **Native HTML `inert` is displacing manual `aria-hidden` toggling** for hiding background content behind an open modal, as browser support has matured through 2024–2026. Technical implementation shift, durable.
- **State-level ADA case law is actively unsettled**, notably California's 2025 near-disappearance of website-accessibility filings due to a state ruling excluding online-only businesses — this is a live, appealable area, not a settled trend (see Open Questions).

## Anti-patterns

- **Placeholder-only fields with no visible label.** Fails NNGroup usability testing on multiple axes and risks non-conformance with WCAG 3.3.2/1.3.1.
- **Confirm-password field as the sole typo-safeguard, with no show/hide toggle offered as an alternative.**
- **CAPTCHA-only login or signup gate with no non-cognitive-test alternative.** Direct conflict with WCAG 3.3.8.
- **Keystroke-level validation that fires before the user finishes typing, or validation withheld entirely until submit.** Both extremes tested worse than on-blur validation with live error-clearing.
- **Color-only error indication** (a red border with no icon or text).
- **Modals without a real focus trap**, where background page content remains tabbable or scrollable behind an open dialog.
- **Marking only optional fields and leaving required fields to be inferred.** Counterintuitively raises the required-field skip rate.
- **Decorative images and icons given descriptive alt text**, which a screen reader reads aloud as noise instead of `alt=""`.
- **A missing skip link, or one that's present in the DOM but not actually the first focusable element.**
- **A sticky header, footer, or cookie-consent banner that visually covers the currently-focused element as a keyboard user tabs down the page** — a direct WCAG 2.2 2.4.11 failure.
- **Blocking clipboard paste into password fields "for security."** Now a WCAG 3.3.8 conformance failure, not just a UX annoyance.

## Open questions

- **Precise field-count-to-conversion percentage curves are unverifiable at the source level.** Digital Applied's table (31.9% CVR at 1 field down to 6.9% at 10+ fields) and the widely-repeated "HubSpot: each added field costs ~4.1% conversion" figure both trace back through chains of marketing blogs that admit — or simply don't disclose — incomplete source mapping across HubSpot/Unbounce/Baymard/Hotjar/Contentsquare. The qualitative direction is robust and repeated everywhere; treat any specific percentage as illustrative, not citable, until traced to one primary study.
- **Multi-step vs. single-step conversion lift (commonly cited as 86–300% higher, or "13.9% vs. 4.5%" per "Formstack") could not be traced to a linkable primary report.** The numbers recirculate through vendor case studies (Formstack, Venture Harbour, Responsify) rather than independent controlled research. Plausible directionally — multi-step forms reduce perceived effort on long/complex asks — but treat the magnitude as unproven.
- **The confirm-password-field-removal recommendation is strong practitioner consensus but thin on primary evidence.** CXL's frequently-cited article returned HTTP 403 on direct fetch and could not be independently verified this pass; no large published controlled study surfaced during this research to confirm the magnitude of the effect, only the direction.
- **Baymard's top-vs-left label-position study is from 2013** (18 sites, 1,000+ fields) — still the most-cited source for "top-aligned labels win," but no more recent large-scale replication was located. The underlying recommendation is durable and consistent with independent eye-tracking (UX Movement/Wroblewski), but the specific study is over a decade old and predates today's responsive-design norms.
- **Total US digital-accessibility lawsuit volume depends heavily on whether state-court filings are included.** Federal-only figures put 2025 website-accessibility suits at 3,117; combined federal + state estimates from the same tracker ecosystem exceed 5,000. Use the federal figure as the defensible, sourced baseline and treat "5,000+" as a directional ceiling, not a precise count.
- **California's near-zero 2025 federal website-suit count is a live legal anomaly, not a stable trend.** It reflects a state-court ruling excluding online-only businesses from California's ADA-adjacent state law, which is subject to appeal; don't build long-term risk assessments assuming California stays a low-exposure jurisdiction.
