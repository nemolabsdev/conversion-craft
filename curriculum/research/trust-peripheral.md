## Rules

**Footer architecture**

1. Group footer links into distinct, separately-labeled semantic sections (e.g., Customer Service, Company, My Account, Legal) rather than one undifferentiated link list — 13% of large e-commerce sites currently have usability issues traceable to poor footer grouping. [FRAMEWORK] — Baymard
2. Always include direct footer links to Return Policy and Shipping Information specifically (not buried inside a general "Help" page) — 20% of sites omit these, and both directly affect purchase-decision confidence. [FRAMEWORK] — Baymard
3. Keep section boundaries semantically clean: a link should belong unambiguously to one heading, not straddle two (e.g., "Shipping" living under both "Customer Service" and "Company"). Fuzzy category boundaries are a named failure mode in Baymard's audit. [FRAMEWORK] — Baymard
4. On mobile, collapse footer sections into accordions but keep the semantic headings visible/tappable at all times — heading legibility matters more on mobile precisely because there is less surrounding space to signal grouping. [FRAMEWORK] — Baymard
5. Give every footer link and its surrounding fine print (copyright line, legal text) a minimum 4.5:1 contrast ratio for normal-size text (3:1 if ≥18pt/14pt-bold) and a minimum 24×24 CSS px tap target on touch devices. [LAW] — WCAG 1.4.3 (AA), WCAG 2.5.8 (AA)
6. Place the CCPA "Do Not Sell or Share My Personal Information" link (and, if sensitive personal information is processed, "Limit the Use of My Sensitive Personal Information") somewhere persistently reachable on the homepage — the footer is the de facto standard location and satisfies the "clear and conspicuous" requirement in practice. [LAW] — CPRA

**Cookie consent banner**

7. Give "Accept" and "Reject" equal visual weight (same size, same color saturation, same layer of the banner) and equal click-depth (one click reaches either outcome) — this is the single most consistently cited requirement across EU supervisory authorities and the specific factor named in CNIL's December 2024 formal notices. [LAW] — EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce, CNIL enforcement
8. Never pre-tick any consent checkbox, including "necessary-adjacent" categories like analytics or personalization — pre-ticked boxes are treated as no consent at all, not weak consent, under both the EDPB Taskforce findings and general GDPR consent standards. [LAW] — EDPB Cookie Banner Taskforce
9. Do not require more steps/clicks to reject than to accept (e.g., "Accept All" as a one-click banner button vs. "Reject" requiring a click into settings, then multiple per-category toggles, then a save action) — named explicitly as a dark pattern in EU enforcement guidance and the direct basis for the largest 2024–2025 CNIL fines (Google €325M, Shein €150M for cookie violations). [LAW] — CNIL, EDPB
10. If the banner is implemented as a modal, make it fully keyboard-operable: focus must move into it on open, Tab/Shift+Tab must cycle only within it while open, and Escape (or an equally reachable close control) must be able to exit it. [LAW] — WCAG 2.1.2
11. Cookie/consent and legal-notice interstitials are explicitly exempt from Google's intrusive-interstitial ranking demotion — but promotional popups are not, so do not disguise a marketing offer as part of the same consent flow to borrow that exemption. [LAW] — Google Search Central intrusive-interstitials guidance
12. Cap the banner's initial footprint: keep it to a bottom or corner bar rather than a full-viewport takeover wherever the applicable regulator's guidance allows a non-blocking presentation — full-page cookie interstitials combine two irritant patterns (blocked content + forced choice) that independently rank among the most-negatively-rated ad-like UI patterns tested. [FRAMEWORK] — NN/g most-hated-techniques research (applied by analogy from popup-ad findings, not a cookie-banner-specific study — see Open Questions)

**Email capture and newsletter popups**

13. Delay any promotional/newsletter popup by at least 10 seconds or until 30% scroll depth (whichever comes first) as a baseline floor — this threshold doubles as the safe-harbor pattern for avoiding Google's mobile intrusive-interstitial ranking demotion, not just a UX nicety. [LAW] — Google intrusive-interstitials guidance
14. Within that floor, tune timing upward for effect: a 6–10 second delay measurably outperforms sub-1-second immediate display (2.4% vs 1.9% conversion in a 1.24B-impression dataset), and triggering after the visitor's second pageview outperforms both (28.98% conversion) — treat "second page viewed" as a stronger intent signal than any fixed timer. [TREND-2026] — Omnisend 2025 popup-display analysis
15. Always ship a visible, immediately-actionable close control on any popup — absence of a close button is one of the single most negatively-rated UI failures measured (93% negative rating), on par with fully blocking page content. [LAW] — NN/g most-hated-advertising-techniques research
16. Do not stack an exit-intent popup on top of a timed/scroll popup shown earlier in the same session — exit-intent triggers convert at the lowest rate of common trigger types in aggregate data (1.8–1.9% vs. 2.1%+ for scroll/pageview triggers) and read to already-departing users as a last-resort interruption; if using exit-intent, suppress it entirely for sessions that already saw another popup. [FRAMEWORK] — Omnisend 2025 data
17. Never pre-check a marketing-consent checkbox bundled with an unrelated required action (account creation, order placement) — under the EU Consumer Rights Directive, pre-ticked boxes for anything with a cost or obligation attached are legally void and refundable/reversible by the consumer; the same "no pre-ticking" standard applies to marketing consent generally under GDPR-adjacent guidance. [LAW] — EU Consumer Rights Directive Art. 22
18. For emailing existing customers without a fresh popup opt-in, the UK/EU "soft opt-in" exemption applies only if all four conditions hold: (a) the email was collected during a sale or sale negotiation, (b) the marketing is for similar products/services only, (c) the customer was given a clear opt-out chance at collection, and (d) every subsequent email offers opt-out again. A newsletter-signup capture or a competition entry does not qualify as "the course of a sale" — those still need explicit consent. [LAW] — PECR Reg 22(3) / ICO guidance
19. Prefer an embedded/inline signup form over a modal popup on pages where the visitor has already shown topic-specific intent (e.g., mid-article, a dedicated resource page) — inline forms convert at roughly double the rate of modal popups in matched high-intent placements (45.5% vs 25.96%) and are reported to deliver higher-quality subscribers with lower unsubscribe/complaint rates. Reserve modal popups for lower-intent, broad-traffic pages where reach matters more than list quality. [FRAMEWORK] — comparative inline-vs-popup testing (context-dependent — see Open Questions)
20. If using a percentage-vs-flat-dollar discount incentive, default to a percentage off for items priced under ~$100 and a flat-dollar amount for items priced over ~$100 — the discount that produces the visually larger number tends to read as more valuable at each price tier ("Rule of 100"). Treat this as a starting heuristic, not a load-bearing finding: controlled 10%/15%/20% comparisons show minimal conversion difference at typical e-commerce price points. [FRAMEWORK] — pricing-psychology heuristic; weak/mixed evidence, see Open Questions
21. Do not default to double opt-in believing it is legally required — GDPR/UK GDPR mandate single opt-in with an affirmative, unticked action; double opt-in is optional best practice for building an audit-proof consent record, not a compliance floor. [LAW] — GDPR consent requirements (Art. 7 record-keeping), not a hard opt-in mechanism mandate

**Order confirmation / thank-you page**

22. Keep the order summary (order number, itemized list with thumbnails, shipping address, delivery estimate, total charged) as the first thing visible on the page, above any cross-sell, survey, or account-creation content — Baymard is explicit that these additions must never displace, only supplement, the core confirmation. [FRAMEWORK] — Baymard
23. Defer account creation to the confirmation page rather than forcing it pre-checkout — this removes a decision point from the purchase path itself while still capturing the account at a moment the customer has already committed. [FRAMEWORK] — Baymard
24. Use the confirmation page, not the checkout flow, for newsletter opt-in and cross-sell offers — post-purchase placement has materially more layout space to explain value than a checkout step optimized for speed, and doesn't add friction to an in-progress transaction. [FRAMEWORK] — Baymard
25. If offering a post-purchase cross-sell ("Add to your order"), let it complete via the already-stored payment method with no re-entry of card details — re-authentication friction is the main usability cost that turns a cross-sell into an abandonment risk at this stage. [FRAMEWORK] — Baymard / post-purchase upsell UX literature

**Post-purchase upsell ethics**

26. Present every post-purchase add-on as an explicit, actively-selected offer (a distinct "Add this" button with its own value proposition) — never pre-select a paid add-on or bundle it into the default total the customer is about to confirm. This mirrors the EU's pre-ticked-box prohibition and is good practice even in jurisdictions where it is not separately codified. [LAW] — EU Consumer Rights Directive Art. 22 (direct rule for EU buyers; sound practice generally)
27. Do not build subscription or recurring-charge upsells around a "roach motel" pattern — easy one-click signup paired with deliberately hard cancellation (multi-step flows, required phone calls, guilt-messaging screens). This is the FTC's and multiple state UDAP regulators' named target pattern for negative-option enforcement, independent of the 2025 vacatur of the specific federal "click-to-cancel" rule. [LAW] — FTC Section 5 / ROSCA / state UDAP laws
28. Treat the FTC's federal "click-to-cancel" rule as currently non-binding (vacated by the 8th Circuit, July 2025) but do not treat cancellation friction as low-risk as a result — ROSCA, FTC Act Section 5, and a majority of state autorenewal statutes independently require cancellation to be at least as easy as signup, and several states enforce this more strictly than the vacated federal rule would have. [LAW] — ROSCA, state autorenewal statutes

**Trust badges and seals**

29. Place security/trust seals directly adjacent to payment fields (inside or immediately bordering the card-entry area), not in the footer or a separate "why trust us" section — Baymard's payment-form research and CXL's placement testing independently converge on payment-field-adjacent placement as what actually moves behavior; footer placement is essentially invisible at the decision moment. [FRAMEWORK] — Baymard, CXL (CXL cited via secondary sources — see Open Questions)
30. Use only 1–2 trust icons within the payment-field area, not a long badge row — Baymard's testing found no benefit past a couple of icons, and other sourcing on badge density suggests a long badge strip reads as compensating/desperate rather than reassuring past 3–4 distinct badge types. [FRAMEWORK] — Baymard; badge-density finding is lower-confidence, vendor-sourced — see Open Questions
31. Choose recognizable, high-familiarity seals over technically-superior-but-unfamiliar ones: across seven independent Baymard surveys (2013–2023) the Norton seal ranked #1 for perceived trust every single time, and was measured as roughly 20x more trust-inducing than lesser-known-but-equally-valid SSL vendor seals (Thawte, GeoTrust). An unfamiliar seal can read as more suspicious than no seal at all. [FRAMEWORK] — Baymard (7-survey longitudinal)
32. Visually encapsulate the payment section of the checkout form (border, background shade, or card-style container that sets it apart from surrounding fields) — this reads as a security signal on its own, independent of any badge placed inside it, and remains a gap: as of Baymard's most recent measurement roughly two-thirds of major US sites still don't apply this treatment. [FRAMEWORK] — Baymard
33. Do not let layout bugs or visual glitches appear anywhere near the payment step — Baymard's qualitative testing found users attribute even minor rendering issues at checkout to hacking or site compromise, sometimes abandoning at well-known, high-trust retailers as a result. Treat payment-adjacent visual QA as a trust-and-conversion issue, not just a polish issue. [FRAMEWORK] — Baymard

## Trends 2024-2026

- **Enforcement, not just guidance, is now driving cookie-banner design.** 2024–2025 saw CNIL move from formal notices to nine-figure fines (Google €325M, Shein €150M) specifically over banner dark patterns, following €139M+ in combined prior fines. Equal-prominence Accept/Reject is no longer a "best practice" recommendation — it is an actively litigated/fined requirement in the EU. **Shelf life: durable** — this is regulatory direction, not a design fad, and other jurisdictions (UK ICO, incoming EU ePrivacy Regulation) are converging on the same standard.
- **US state privacy law is fragmenting rather than consolidating.** Indiana, Kentucky, and Rhode Island add comprehensive privacy laws effective January 1, 2026; Texas, Oregon, Delaware, and Connecticut are separately expanding opt-out/consent obligations; over 20 states now use an opt-out (vs. EU opt-in) consent model. A single banner design cannot be "US-compliant" as a monolith — it needs geo-aware logic. **Shelf life: durable, likely to intensify** through 2026–2027 as more states phase in laws.
- **Global Privacy Control (GPC) and other machine-readable opt-out signals are becoming mandatory to honor, not just polite to support.** California has required GPC compliance since March 2024; more states are following. This shifts consent-signal handling from a banner-only UI problem to a backend/header-detection problem. **Shelf life: durable** — this is now settled law in the largest US privacy jurisdiction.
- **Google reversed its third-party-cookie deprecation plan (April 2025)**, keeping third-party cookies on by default in Chrome with user-level opt-out, while 17–20% of global traffic is cookieless anyway via Safari/Firefox/Brave defaults. Consent Mode v2 has become a de facto industry standard for the EU/UK, but audits report roughly two-thirds of Consent Mode v2 implementations still fail to meet compliance bars. **Shelf life: volatile** — the cookie-deprecation timeline itself has already reversed once; treat any "cookies are going away" design premise as unreliable through 2026.
- **Gamified capture mechanics (spin-to-win, scratch cards) are substantially outperforming static discount popups** in current large-sample data (single-digit-percent conversion for standard popups vs. high-single/low-double-digit percent for gamified formats). This is popular-press/vendor-dataset evidence (Wisepops, Claspo, Omnisend), not peer-reviewed or Baymard/NN/g-grade research. **Shelf life: uncertain** — could be a genuine mechanic or a novelty effect that decays as gamified popups saturate the web; re-verify before treating the multiplier as durable.
- **Post-purchase/negative-option cancellation friction remains a live regulatory target even after the federal "click-to-cancel" rule's vacatur.** The FTC has moved to revive rulemaking, and ROSCA/state UDAP enforcement continues independent of that specific rule's fate. Design teams should not read the 2025 vacatur as "cancellation friction is fine now." **Shelf life: durable enforcement risk**, unstable rule text — watch for a re-issued or narrower FTC rule in 2026–2027.

## Anti-patterns

- **Unequal-prominence cookie banners** — a large, colored "Accept All" button paired with a plain-text or low-contrast "Reject"/"Manage Preferences" link. Named directly in CNIL's 2024 formal notices and the single most cited EDPB Taskforce finding.
- **Pre-ticked consent or add-on checkboxes** of any kind — marketing consent, analytics cookies, paid add-ons/insurance/warranties bundled into checkout defaults. Legally void for paid items under EU Consumer Rights Directive Art. 22 and treated as no-consent under GDPR generally.
- **Cookie/consent flows requiring more clicks or steps to reject than to accept** (e.g., one-click Accept vs. click-into-settings-then-toggle-each-category-then-save to Reject).
- **Full-viewport, unclosable popups with no visible close control** — the specific combination (blocks content + no close button) both independently rank among the most-negatively-rated UI patterns ever tested by NN/g (93% negative each).
- **Immediate-on-load promotional popups** (0–1 second delay) — measurably lower-converting than a delayed trigger and squarely inside Google's intrusive-interstitial penalty zone if on mobile search traffic.
- **Stacking multiple popup types in one session** (e.g., a scroll popup followed later by an exit-intent popup) — compounds annoyance without a compounding conversion benefit; exit-intent specifically converts at the lowest rate of common triggers.
- **"Roach motel" cancellation flows** — one-click subscribe, multi-step/phone-required/guilt-messaged cancel. The canonical negative-option dark pattern named by the FTC and state regulators.
- **Trust-badge rows using unfamiliar or homemade-looking SSL/security seals**, or stacking more than 3–4 distinct badge types — unfamiliar seals can read as more suspicious than no seal, and badge-dense checkout footers read as compensating rather than reassuring.
- **Footer link lists with no semantic grouping/headings**, or grouping so loose that a link's category is ambiguous — the named cause of Baymard's 13% footer-usability-issue figure.
- **Order confirmation pages that bury or omit the core order summary** in favor of cross-sell/survey/promo content — Baymard is explicit this must never happen; the confirmation's primary job is confirming the order.

## Open questions

- **CXL's original trust-seal recognition study (McAfee 79%, VeriSign 76%, PayPal 72%) could not be independently verified by direct fetch** — cxl.com returned HTTP 403 to automated fetch. These figures are widely repeated across secondary sources but should be re-verified against CXL directly (or Baymard Premium, which appears to be the primary source CXL itself draws from) before being presented as a fully independently-confirmed number.
- **The "more than 3–4 badge types decreases conversion 5–8%" and "62% conversion increase after removing 11 badges" figures trace to vendor/case-study blog content, not a controlled Baymard/NN/g study.** Directionally plausible given the broader "unfamiliar seal reads as suspicious" finding, but treat the specific percentages as marketing claims pending independent replication.
- **NN/g's most-hated-advertising-techniques data (95% negative rating on pop-ups, etc.) is a 2004 study of banner/pop-up ADS specifically, not a cookie-banner or newsletter-popup study.** Applying its close-button and content-blocking findings to modern cookie banners and email-capture modals is a reasonable analogy (same UI mechanism, same interruption structure) but is not itself a study of those specific surface types — no equivalently rigorous, recent NN/g-grade study of newsletter-popup or cookie-banner-specific irritation was found in this pass.
- **Inline-vs-modal conversion comparisons are highly context-dependent and not resolved into one ranking.** The 45.5% vs 25.96% figure is from a single comparative test on high-intent pages; general benchmarks elsewhere show modal popups (4.44–7.39%) outperforming typical inline placements sitewide. The honest read is "it depends on page intent and placement," not a universal inline > modal rule — treat any single-number claim in either direction skeptically.
- **The "Rule of 100" (percentage-vs-dollar discount framing) is a well-known pricing-psychology heuristic, not something this pass found validated by a controlled A/B study specific to popup incentive design.** The one controlled data point found (10%/15%/20% showing minimal difference) partially contradicts the heuristic's practical importance. Treat as a reasonable starting default, test before relying on it.
- **Gamified popup conversion-multiplier claims (up to 642% lift) come exclusively from popup-vendor-published datasets (Wisepops, Claspo, Omnisend), each of which sells the tool being measured.** No independent, non-vendor replication was found. Directionally credible (novelty + interactivity plausibly drive engagement) but treat the magnitude as vendor-marketing-grade evidence, not research-grade.
- **No current, direct-fetched Baymard or NN/g source specifically addresses sitemap-page necessity/placement** (as distinct from footer link architecture generally) — search results returned only secondary/tertiary blog commentary on sitemaps. This sub-topic is under-evidenced in this research pass relative to the rest of the footer section.
