## Rules

**Order confirmation**

1. Send the order-confirmation email immediately (seconds to low minutes) after checkout completes — it is the highest-open-rate message in the entire lifecycle because the buyer is actively checking it, with reported ranges of 60–90% depending on measurement methodology. [FRAMEWORK] — shno.co (aggregating MagicBell/Salesforce), corroborated directionally by Klaviyo's post-purchase guide
2. Keep the confirmation's job singular: order summary, itemized pricing, and an expected-delivery window. Treat it as a trust/reassurance surface, not a hard-sell surface — cross-sell belongs in a later, separate touch (see Rule 15), not stapled onto the confirmation. [FRAMEWORK] — Klaviyo post-purchase guide

**Shipping & delivery notifications**

3. Send the shipping-confirmation the moment a tracking number is generated, not on a batch schedule. Reported open rates for this message type range 45–63% across sources that disagree on methodology — treat the range as directional, not a hard target, and validate against your own list. [FRAMEWORK] — WebSearch synthesis of shipping-notification benchmarks (unresolved variance across sources; see Open Questions)
4. When a shipment is delayed, send a proactive delay notice (updated ETA + brief acknowledgment) rather than waiting for the customer to open a support ticket. This is vendor-reported behavior data (not independently verified third-party research) but the mechanism is low-risk to adopt regardless: acknowledge before being asked. [FRAMEWORK] — LateShipment.com vendor data; treat percentages as unverified (see Open Questions)
5. Route "where is my order" (WISMO) volume reduction through proactive status emails rather than support headcount — WISMO-type inquiries are reported to be a large (30–50%) share of ecommerce support ticket volume during peak periods. [FRAMEWORK] — LateShipment.com vendor data

**Abandoned-cart recovery**

6. Trigger the first cart-recovery email within roughly 30–60 minutes of abandonment. Sending within the first hour is reported to meaningfully outperform sending at the 24-hour mark on recovery, even holding copy constant. [FRAMEWORK] — WebSearch synthesis (attribuly.com, beyondopenrate.com.au); directionally consistent with Klaviyo's "2–4 hours" first-touch window
7. Build a 3-email sequence as the baseline structure: a plain reminder at 2–4 hours, a follow-up (optionally with an incentive) at ~24 hours, and a final message at ~48 hours featuring product recommendations or a lower-pressure nudge. [FRAMEWORK] — Klaviyo abandoned-cart benchmark report
8. Do not lead with a discount in email #1. A meaningful share of cart abandonment is caused by distraction, payment friction, or price-checking rather than pure price sensitivity — a plain reminder alone recovers carts abandoned for these non-price reasons without giving away margin unnecessarily. [FRAMEWORK] — synthesis of Klaviyo sequence guidance + margin-erosion analysis (margly.io); treat as directional risk-management principle, not a measured A/B result
9. Benchmark program health against Klaviyo's 2024 dataset: average abandoned-cart flow performance is ~50.5% open / 6.25% click / 3.33% conversion / $3.65 revenue-per-recipient (RPR); top-decile performers reach ~65.3% open / 13.3% click / 7.7% conversion / $28.89 RPR. [FRAMEWORK] — Klaviyo abandoned-cart benchmark report (verified by direct fetch)
10. Expect RPR to scale with average order value: Klaviyo's data shows $7.01 RPR for stores with $100–200 AOV vs. $14.14 RPR for stores with $200+ AOV — do not apply a single flat RPR target across product categories with different price points. [FRAMEWORK] — Klaviyo ecommerce-benchmarks page (note: underlying study is from Q4 2016 per that page's own methodology note — use for the directional AOV relationship, not as a current absolute figure; see Open Questions)
11. Cap discount-escalation depth against your margin, not against "whatever converts." As an illustrative floor: at a 30% gross margin, a 10% discount already consumes roughly a third of the unit's profit — decide your escalation ceiling before the sequence goes live, not email-by-email in response to conversion pressure. [FRAMEWORK] — margly.io margin-erosion analysis
12. Never pair a cart-recovery discount with fabricated urgency or scarcity signals (fake countdown timers, invented "only 2 left" claims) — this is squarely inside the conduct the FTC's 2022 dark-patterns report and subsequent enforcement actions target. [LAW] — FTC "Bringing Dark Patterns to Light" (Sept. 2022)

**Browse abandonment**

13. Trigger browse-abandonment flows off a product-page view with no add-to-cart event, but calibrate expectations downward: reported click-to-conversion for browse abandonment sits around 1.5–3%, versus >3% conversion commonly reported for cart abandonment — the funnel position is earlier and less committed. [FRAMEWORK] — WebSearch synthesis (beyondopenrate.com.au, 624agency.com)
14. Keep browse-abandonment copy informational/helpful by default (e.g., "still thinking about this?" plus supporting content) rather than discount-led; browse-stage RPR in Klaviyo's flow-ranking data sits well below both abandoned-cart and welcome-series RPR, so it does not carry the same margin budget for aggressive incentives. [FRAMEWORK] — Klaviyo ecommerce-benchmarks page (Q4 2016 methodology — directional ranking only, see Open Questions)

**Review requests**

15. Trigger review-request sends off the confirmed **delivery** date, not the order date — product category should set the exact lag (electronics ≈7–10 days post-delivery to allow setup/initial use; skincare/beauty ≈14–21 days post-delivery to allow time-to-result). [FRAMEWORK] — WebSearch synthesis (ravecapture.com); the specific claim that delivery-synced triggers lift response 2–3% to as high as 39% could not be traced to a named methodology and should be treated as unverified (see Open Questions)
16. Route review requests to a distinct flow from cross-sell/upsell — Klaviyo's own guidance explicitly sequences "after delivery, after the customer has had a chance to use it" for reviews as a separate trigger from the upsell flow. [FRAMEWORK] — Klaviyo post-purchase guide

**Win-back / reactivation**

17. Set the win-back trigger relative to each customer's own repurchase cadence, not a fixed calendar day: a commonly cited heuristic is ~1.5x the customer's average repurchase interval for consumables, and 90–120 days of inactivity for non-consumable/durable categories. [FRAMEWORK] — Klaviyo win-back guide (quoting practitioner Jacob Sappington) + WebSearch synthesis
18. Structure win-back as a short escalating sequence (2–5 messages over 1–2 weeks), moving from a soft "we miss you" reminder toward a time-boxed incentive in the final message — but note explicitly that Klaviyo's own write-up states this range is best-practice consensus, **not** backed by published conversion data in that source. [FRAMEWORK] — Klaviyo win-back guide (self-disclosed as non-data-backed)
19. Do not cite a specific "Xx higher conversion" figure for early- vs. late-triggered win-back sends (e.g., a commonly repeated "2.3x at 30–45 days vs. 90+ days" claim) in client-facing material without your own test data — this figure could not be traced to any named primary study during this research pass. [FRAMEWORK] — flagged as unverifiable; see Open Questions

**Unsubscribe & frequency hygiene**

20. Build a dedicated sunset flow, capped at 3 emails, for subscribers who have gone unengaged (no opens/clicks) over a defined window sized to your normal send frequency — Klaviyo's own official documentation specifies this 3-email ceiling to avoid damaging deliverability by repeatedly contacting unengaged addresses. [FRAMEWORK] — Klaviyo Help Center, "How to create a sunset flow" (verified by direct fetch)
21. Exclude (and re-activate) anyone who opens or clicks at any point during the sunset flow — only tag and suppress the addresses that remain silent through the full flow. [FRAMEWORK] — Klaviyo Help Center, "How to create a sunset flow"
22. Suppress — do not merely stop targeting — addresses that complete the sunset flow with zero engagement; continuing to include them in general sends measurably damages sender reputation and inbox placement for the rest of the list. [FRAMEWORK] — Klaviyo Help Center, "How to create a sunset flow" / "Understanding active profile management"

**Consent boundaries — CAN-SPAM (US)**

23. Order confirmations, shipping updates, and other messages whose primary purpose is completing an already-agreed transaction are exempt from CAN-SPAM's opt-out mandate as "transactional or relationship" messages — but if commercial content is mixed in and a reasonable recipient's subject-line read, or the ordering of content (commercial content appearing before the transactional content), would suggest a commercial purpose, the message is treated as commercial and the full opt-out/labeling rules apply. [LAW] — FTC CAN-SPAM rule (16 CFR Part 316), via convergent secondary legal sources; not independently fetched from ftc.gov this pass (see Sources)
24. Honor every opt-out/unsubscribe request within 10 business days, and never charge a fee, require login, or require more information than an email address to process it. Every commercial email must carry a functioning opt-out mechanism and the sender's valid physical postal address. [LAW] — FTC CAN-SPAM rule, via convergent secondary legal sources
25. Treat CAN-SPAM penalty exposure as per-recipient, not per-campaign: the FTC's current inflation-adjusted civil-penalty cap is reported at $53,088 per individual violating email. Confirm this figure against ftc.gov directly before using it in a legal-risk brief — it was not independently fetched this pass. [LAW] — convergent secondary legal sources (Kelley Drye, LegalClarity, Tomba, Adaptive Security); primary FTC page blocked to direct fetch this pass

**Consent boundaries — GDPR / ePrivacy (EU/UK)**

26. Abandoned-cart emails do not automatically require fresh opt-in consent — the ePrivacy Directive/PECR "soft opt-in" (PECR Reg 22(3), ePrivacy Art. 13(2)) permits sending them without prior consent, but only when **all three** conditions hold simultaneously: (a) the email address was collected in the context of a sale or sale-negotiation (i.e., the shopper reached the email-capture step of checkout), (b) the marketing content is scoped only to the abandoned item(s) or similar products, and (c) a clear opt-out was offered both at the point of collection and in every subsequent message. [LAW] — gdpr-advisor.com legal explainer, citing PECR/ePrivacy text
27. If a shopper abandoned before reaching the email-capture field (e.g., the address was sourced from a retargeting pixel, a co-registration list, or a previous unrelated purchase), the soft-opt-in basis does not apply to that address for this cart event — using it anyway without a separate valid legal basis risks a PECR/GDPR violation. The ICO's 2021 £200,000 fine against We Buy Any Car Ltd is a live enforcement precedent for exactly this failure mode (opt-out offered only in later messages, not "at the exact moment" details were entered). [LAW] — gdpr-advisor.com, citing the ICO enforcement action
28. Keep abandoned-cart and browse-abandonment marketing content strictly scoped to the abandoned/viewed items or genuinely similar products — broadening the content to unrelated promotions forfeits the soft-opt-in legal basis entirely, even if the other two conditions were met. [LAW] — gdpr-advisor.com legal explainer
29. Where legitimate interest (GDPR Art. 6(1)(f)) is used instead of soft opt-in or consent, document a three-part Legitimate Interest Assessment (Purpose Test, Necessity Test, Balancing Test) before the flow goes live, and re-run it whenever the flow's targeting or content scope changes materially. [LAW] — gdpr-advisor.com legal explainer
30. Define and enforce a documented retention period for abandoned-cart/browse data; indefinite retention of this behavioral data violates GDPR's storage-limitation principle regardless of which lawful basis (consent, soft opt-in, or legitimate interest) underlies the emails themselves. [LAW] — gdpr-advisor.com legal explainer
31. Understand the fine exposure tier: GDPR consent and direct-marketing violations (Art. 5–7, 9) fall under the **upper** administrative-fine tier — up to €20,000,000 or 4% of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher — not the lower €10M/2% tier reserved for procedural/documentation-obligation breaches. [LAW] — gdpr-info.eu, Article 83 (verified by direct fetch)

**Measurement hygiene**

32. Do not treat open rate as a reliable success metric for any flow in this lifecycle on its own — Apple Mail Privacy Protection (MPP) auto-triggers opens for a large share of iOS/macOS Mail traffic regardless of whether a human read the message, inflating every open-rate figure cited above by an unknown, non-uniform amount. Pair open rate with click rate and conversion/RPR before making a flow-level call. [FRAMEWORK] — WebSearch synthesis of 2025–2026 email-benchmark methodology notes (widely corroborated pattern, not tied to one named study)

## Anti-patterns

- **Discount on touch #1.** Leading the very first cart-recovery message with a coupon teaches price-insensitive abandoners (payment friction, distraction, comparison shopping) that abandoning is a free way to unlock a discount, training future abandon-for-savings behavior and eroding margin on recoveries that would have converted at full price anyway.
- **Escalating the discount every single email regardless of engagement.** A fixed escalation ladder (10% → 15% → 20%) ignores whether the recipient has even opened prior messages, maximizes margin giveaway on customers who were going to buy anyway, and sets a floor expectation for the next purchase cycle too.
- **Fake urgency inside recovery emails.** Countdown timers with no real expiration, "only 2 left in stock" claims not tied to real inventory, or "your cart expires in 1 hour" messaging not backed by an actual reservation system — this is the exact conduct category the FTC's dark-patterns enforcement targets.
- **Mislabeling marketing content as transactional to dodge CAN-SPAM's opt-out rule.** Stuffing promotional content into what's nominally a "shipping update" or "order confirmation" to avoid the unsubscribe-link requirement fails the FTC's primary-purpose test the moment content ordering or subject-line framing tips commercial.
- **Broadening soft-opt-in scope.** Using an abandoned-cart address (collected under the ePrivacy soft-opt-in) to send general newsletter content or unrelated-category promotions — this forfeits the legal basis for the whole relationship, not just the out-of-scope message.
- **Hiding the unsubscribe/cancel path behind multiple clicks or decoy links.** The FTC's ABCmouse enforcement example — a cancellation flow requiring navigation through several promotional pages before reaching the actual cancel action — is a documented, punished dark pattern, not a gray area.
- **Continuing to send marketing to addresses that finished a sunset flow with zero engagement.** This actively damages sender reputation and inbox placement for the entire list, not just the unengaged segment.
- **Triggering review requests off order date instead of delivery date.** Asking before the product has arrived (let alone been used) collects fewer, lower-quality reviews and burns the one good "please review" touch a customer will tolerate.
- **One-size-fits-all win-back timing.** Applying the same 60–90-day dormancy window to a weekly-consumable subscription and a once-a-decade durable-goods purchase misreads what "lapsed" even means for that customer.
- **Reporting open rate alone as the success metric for any of these flows.** Given Apple MPP's inflation effect, an open-rate-only readout can make a genuinely underperforming flow look healthy.

## Open questions

- **Omnisend 2026 report figures (76% of automation revenue, $3.59 RPR, 42.02% click-to-conversion, $168 AOV, 22.5% adoption) are unverified by direct fetch.** The omnisend.com domain was blocked by this environment's fetch tool; these numbers come only from WebSearch's crawled snippets of the report, not a first-party read. Re-verify by fetching the report directly (via a different network path, PDF download, or a syndicated republish) before treating these as load-bearing benchmarks.
- **The "RetailMeNot: 80% of shoppers who get a cart-abandonment discount deliberately abandon future carts to trigger another one" claim could not be traced to any locatable RetailMeNot publication.** It surfaced only inside a secondary blog's synthesis with no working citation. Treat as unverifiable/likely-miscited until a primary source is found — do not repeat it as fact.
- **The review-request "delivery-synced trigger lifts response 2–3% → up to 39% (13x)" claim has no traceable methodology, sample size, or named study.** Directionally plausible (delivery-date triggers should outperform order-date triggers) but the magnitude is unverified.
- **The win-back "sent at 30–45 days converts at 2.3x the rate of sends at 90+ days" claim has no traceable primary source**, and Klaviyo's own win-back guide explicitly disclaims having conversion data behind its timing recommendations. Needs a named study or should be dropped from any claim set.
- **WISMO/shipping-notification impact statistics (60–70% ticket reduction, 18% repeat-purchase lift, 69% "won't buy again," 58% churn after 3 delays) all trace back to vendor blogs (LateShipment and similar) reporting on their own customers**, not independent third-party research. Directionally credible but should be labeled as vendor-sourced whenever cited externally.
- **Shipping-confirmation open-rate benchmarks disagree meaningfully across sources (45% vs. ~62–63%)** with no shared methodology visible. Needs a single controlled benchmark source (e.g., a platform's own aggregated-send data, fetched directly) to resolve.
- **Klaviyo's flow-to-flow RPR ranking (abandoned cart > welcome > browse abandonment > winback) is sourced to a page whose own stated methodology is a Q4 2016 study** — current as an ordinal ranking, questionable as an absolute-dollar reference nearly a decade later. Klaviyo appears not to have republished an updated version of this specific flow-comparison table; worth checking whether their newer benchmark reports (e.g., the 2024 abandoned-cart-specific report already used above) have superseded it for welcome/browse/winback RPR specifically.
- **CAN-SPAM's $53,088-per-email penalty figure and the 10-business-day opt-out window were not confirmed against ftc.gov directly** (403 on direct fetch; ecfr.gov redirected to an unresolvable interstitial). Corroborated by multiple independent legal-secondary sources converging on the same numbers, which raises confidence, but this should be re-verified against the primary FTC/eCFR text before it appears in any compliance-facing document.
- **SMS-channel consent (TCPA in the US) for cart-recovery sequences was out of this research's requested scope (GDPR/CAN-SPAM only) but is increasingly part of the actual sequence design** (email + SMS layered flows). If SMS is going to be part of the delivered curriculum, TCPA consent rules need a dedicated research pass — they are materially different from CAN-SPAM's email rules (prior express written consent requirements, different penalty structure).
